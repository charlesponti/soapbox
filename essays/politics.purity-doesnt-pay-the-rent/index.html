Purity Doesn't Pay the Rent

Why anti-AI purity is a status badge the elite preach and the underfunded pay for in full

  • ai
  • status
  • creativity
  • class
  • hollywood

First light on a building site, and the crane is already set, its cab hanging high over the lot, while the crew below works the ground with shovels and keeps glancing up at the machine they are told not to trust. The foreman sits in the cab with the window rolled down.

The shovel has a second purpose: it keeps the laborer too busy to look up, and it makes the labor look like a morality.

Walk the site and the whole arrangement sits in one frame: the purists on the ground are told that hand-shoveling is the authentic way, that the crane is soulless, that real work happens with a shovel, while the foreman watches from the cab. Watch how fast the foreman starts praising the authentic shoveler. The praise is cheap, and the crane is already paid for.

The Rules the Rich Can Afford

In Los Angeles the purity rules are preached on panels and enforced in friend groups, and the people who set them can afford every cost they impose. A wealthy, connected writer who refuses AI is fine; she has a safety net, a trust fund, or a network of family friends who will get her a meeting anyway. The working-class writer struggling to pay rent who adopts the same stance is committing career suicide. Same costume, one paycheck apart.

The rules are a tax on people without savings.

The elite are not deluded; they are setting up a game where the rules they publicly preach keep the competition from competing effectively. The showrunner with a three-picture deal does not do his own administrative work, formatting, research, or outlines — a staff of three junior writers and two assistants does the heavy lifting. The purity rules are a hiring freeze on everyone else’s advantage.

Your Free Intern

The reframe that breaks the spell is arithmetic. You do not have the money to hire a staff; you are one person trying to compete with a machine of people, and AI is not the corporate boss — it is your free, 24/7 unpaid intern. Refusing it is not a moral victory. It is fighting a team of five with one hand tied behind your back.

Use the tool for the boring parts. Fifty bad logline ideas so you can find the good one, a formatted beat sheet, plot holes in your act transitions. AI is spellcheck that argues back, and spellcheck never made a manuscript soulless. The crane does not care who climbs into the cab, and the foreman’s whole business is making you believe it does.

You will also hear that the tool makes the work soulless; the soullessness was never in the tool, it is in the gap between the people who use the tools and the people who are told not to. The same script ran for the typewriter and the film camera and the word processor.

The hypocrisy is not hidden; it is just uncredited. The people on the panels have production offices that absolutely use AI to summarize scripts, generate coverage, analyze data, and punch up jokes — they just do not put it in the credits, because union optics and brand protection matter. They are happy to watch you starve yourself of the tools, because your starvation is less competition; while you spend three days manually formatting a pitch deck, they used a model in twenty minutes and spent the rest of the time networking.

The Mimicry of a Mimicry

Quiet integration is already the norm at the top. Elite agencies, showrunners, and top-tier production companies feed their workflows to language models for coverage, market analysis, brainstorming, formatting, and pitch decks, and never mention it in public.

Studios do not buy raw machine text; they buy taste, curation, and execution. The writers who use the tools to supercharge their output get hired because they deliver three times faster. The ones refusing to touch it are pricing themselves out of the market.

This is the writer in 1990 refusing the word processor because true writing only happens on a typewriter, and the typewriter never paid anyone’s rent. The secure can afford six months on one pure spec script, because family wealth and deep connections are their real portfolio; the struggling writer lives hand-to-mouth and needs to produce high-quality work at volume just to stay in the game — purity is a luxury good for people with safety nets, and it is being sold at cost to the people without them.

The people who can afford to lose the race write the rules of the race. The people who cannot afford to lose read the rules as the race itself — that is the whole trick, and it works every generation.

Purity does not pay the rent.

You cannot save someone who wants to be martyred for a trend, but the talented ones trapped in a bad mental loop can be shown the difference between a badge and a strategy. The badge keeps the friend group warm; the strategy keeps the lights on. Nobody puts the shovel in your hand except the people who profit from the hand-shoveling.

The crane is not the enemy of the laborer. The enemy is the man selling the shovel while he rides the crane, and the laborer who believes the shovel is what makes the work real.
