Sunsetting Rise

Rise died because its value was invisible, and a solved sliding puzzle looks exactly like an unsolved one

  • startup
  • post-mortem
  • calendar
  • product-design
  • technology

We spent four years solving a puzzle nobody could see us solving. Rise was a calendar that read your schedule, found the fragmented pockets of time, and rearranged the meetings so you got your mornings back, and the harder the product worked, the less there was to notice.

The puzzle is the old four-by-four sliding square, where you move one tile to make space for another. Move the ten o’clock meeting and you free the hour before it; move the standup and you free the hour after, and every move is visible on the board. The board is finite, like a calendar, and the limitation is the feature, not the bug.

A solved puzzle looks exactly like the puzzle before you started.

That is the whole story of the company. We raised a pre-seed round of €2.5 million, built FocusGuard to block off overloaded calendars, built an engine that would optimize schedules without needing your attention or approval, and later built TimeFold, a solver that considers thousands of possible arrangements and picks the best one. The metrics climbed — hundreds of hours reclaimed, thousands of minutes optimized — and the users said the same thing: we do not notice Rise having a positive impact, and people find Rise really annoying. They blamed us for every last-minute change we never made and every conflict we could not resolve. A calendar that works perfectly is a calendar nobody thinks about, and thinking about you was the only way you were going to be adopted.

The Rearrangement

The closer to invisible, the closer to ideal. We wanted the calendar to work flawlessly so teams would trust it and bring everyone in. You cannot optimize a schedule with access to one person; you need the whole team on the board to make real space. The people we moved blamed the mover, and the hours we won back were indistinguishable from the hours that had always been there.

The calendar is a finite board, and we built like people who believed in the board. We synchronized Google, Outlook, and iCloud, shipped Windows and macOS integrations, launched iOS and Android apps, and built working locations, out-of-office events, alerts, attachments, recurring events, keyboard commands, and smart color-coding. It was the collection of small things you just expect from a calendar, and each one was a tile we had to slide, and meanwhile the cohort around us — Cron, Amie, and then Google itself — was sliding the same tiles with an eye for detail that belongs to the baseline now.

We wanted both the calendar and the innovation on top of it, and doing both at once delayed the insight we needed. The insight was about the tiles: teams joined because the product looked good, and stayed because they noticed nothing.

Flexible events were the lesson.

We rolled out flexible events in the fall of 2022 to a handful of teams, and the feedback was disheartening. When the engine moved a meeting, people associated Rise with any bad change in their schedules and never with the time it had won back, and they gradually disabled the feature until we decided it was a dead end. It was the viral effect in reverse — the feature we relied on every day, the one that won our first paying team, was also the one nobody could see working. Chaos is visible; improvement is not.

The Board’s Arithmetic

In 2024 we turned to projects, folding scheduling and task management into one tool, and for the first time the numbers moved. Retention doubled for users who started using tasks, we had around nine hundred teams in various stages of growth, and the pipeline held a few hundred teams in transition. But only a handful would switch and pay, and the runway would not carry the sales push we needed.

So we went fundraising, into what the market calls the death zone. We spoke with roughly eighty funds, flew to London and Berlin, and landed on the wrong side of the curve: early revenue and real traction, none of it strong enough to prove hypergrowth on a single metric.

The rejections shared one message: great product, great vision, lack of commercial traction in a competitive space. It felt unfair, and from a distance it was true. Four years in, the invisible value had a four-year track record of being invisible.

Nobody pays for a solved puzzle.

The Exit

We looked for a destination, and after exploring roughly six promising options across Europe, none would let the product continue, so we weighed an acquihire and decided against dedicating the next years of our careers to someone else’s board. We considered charging individuals, and the arithmetic closed the case: even if every active user paid, it would not cover four engineers. The only path left was slimming the company to two of us and betting our savings on a scale problem we had not solved with six.

We kept Rise running until March 31, 2025, made the export easy, refunded the unused subscriptions, and erased the local copies of calendars and events as promised. The company ended, and the board we solved for four years now looks exactly like it did the day we started.

Nobody claps for a solved puzzle.

The hours we reclaimed were real. We measured them, reported them, and printed them in the emails, and they still did not survive contact with the people they helped, because a rearranged morning feels like the ordinary morning it should have been. The market did not reject the product; it failed to see it, and you cannot subscribe to what you cannot see.

That is the arithmetic no spreadsheet shows: the better you solve a problem, the more it disappears, and the people who might have paid for the solution are the ones who think they never had the problem. The puzzle is finished, and it looks exactly like it looked.
