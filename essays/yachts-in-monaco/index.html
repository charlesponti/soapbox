The Yachts in Monaco

The new politics is alignment, not belief, and the only people who can afford to stand straight are offshore

  • politics
  • los-angeles
  • sociology
  • inequality
  • wealth

The afternoon light in the canyons had gone the color of a bruised peach, heavy with the dry heat that precedes the Santa Anas. We sat in a room where the air conditioning hummed with a low, expensive persistence, and two glasses of water sweated onto small squares of linen. We were talking about France, which is how Los Angeles talks about endings.

The wind was already coming through the passes.

The prompt had been an observation about the density of anti-Jewish sentiment in the French Republic, and we ran the usual academic calculus first: the five hundred thousand Jews who remain, the five or six million Muslims in the concrete banlieues outside Paris and Marseille, the names that have become shorthand for a specific dread — Ilan Halimi, the Toulouse school, the Hyper Cacher supermarket. It did not want to stay in France. France was the lens for a more intimate fragmentation, and my friend put the frame on it: threat alignment. If you live next to the group you fear, you adopt their viewpoint as insurance, and the insurance is a lean into the wind.

In the sociology textbooks they call this group threat theory, hostile walls going up when one group feels the weight of another. What my friend described was more adaptive and more cynical: group A looks at group B, feels their mass, and adopts B’s prejudices against group C to buy a little safety.

The Credentialed Proletariat

He moved on to the graduates, the massive expansion of supply that the culture had promised would end at a ladder. The market wanted credentials in math, science, and engineering, and the rest arrived in the cities to find the ladder pulled up. They trade their cultural capital for numerical mass, aligning themselves with whichever minority gets them resources.

This is what Peter Turchin calls elite overproduction: a society that manufactures more credentialed, ambitious people than its institutions can absorb. You end up with a large, articulate, frustrated class packed into hyper-expensive zip codes, unable to climb the ladders, so they seek to dismantle them, providing the vocabulary while the working class provides the votes. In France the price of admission is hardline anti-Zionism; in America it is a different orthodoxy. The mechanism is identical, a scramble for a seat at a table that keeps getting smaller.

There is nowhere to go, he said, for either side. Both extremes are engaged in a noble resistance that looks a great deal like a refusal to adapt to the present, the left seeing a fascist corporate hegemony and the right seeing a woke conspiracy.

And then he smiled and told me to follow the yachts in Monaco.

The Yachts

The vast majority of very wealthy people are the yachts in Monaco. You have never heard of them or their businesses; they have no political opinions; they want to do their business, have a good life, protect their assets. Big business is amoral, he said, and it will change to whatever is effective and believe whatever sells. The great blind spot of the political imagination is the insistence that elites are wicked or enlightened, when the harder truth is that they are simply indifferent.

Corporate funding of a progressive campaign or a conservative lobbying effort is not conviction; it is a heat-seeking missile for profit and stability. The truly wealthy do not need the password of correct opinions, because their survival does not depend on peer-group approval — they have the yachts, and they have Monaco.

Two Monaco yacht owners will help each other before two baristas will, and you would think it would be the opposite. The baristas live in a world of ideological orthodoxy where one wrong word means excommunication, and they can offer each other nothing but a like on a screen. The yacht owners need no agreement on religion or the good life, only the knowledge that a favor today secures a transaction tomorrow.

The Dry Hillside

The story says scarcity breeds community, that the shared counter and the shared gig create a bond wealth destroys. Abundance allows strategic reciprocity; scarcity creates a zero-sum calculation where your neighbor is the person competing for the last available slot, and the psychological reality is the reverse of the myth.

Community is just another luxury good the middle and lower classes have been priced out of.

People do not arrive at their beliefs through reason; they arrive through culture, through the need to maintain cohesion or secure resources, and Jonathan Haidt’s image is the truest one — the small rider of reason on the elephant of instinct, inventing public relations for whatever the beast just crashed through. The alignment with the threat, the adoption of the script, the furious signaling on the screens: it all looks less like conviction and more like people shivering in the cold around a fire that is already out. Outside, the Santa Ana was blowing hard now, a hot, invisible force coming down through the passes, drying out the hills. The hills were dry, and they were waiting for the first spark.

The yachts are not in the hills; they are offshore, where the wind does not reach, and the indifference that looks like a moral failure from dry ground is simply a better position. The wind aligns everything that stays on the ground. The only people who can afford to stand straight are the ones who left the land.
