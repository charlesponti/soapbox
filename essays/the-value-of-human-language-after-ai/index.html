The Value of Human Language After AI

Most of what people value in language is what it does, not who made it — and AI's real disruption is separating the act of creating from the credit for creating

  • technology
  • language
  • ai
  • creativity
  • provenance

Most of what people value in language is not the fact that a human produced it. They value what the language does. A pot holds water because of its walls and its glaze, not because a named hand threw it.

When my wife tells me she loves me, the speaker is the message.

If two passages are identical, the fact that one was written by a person and the other generated by a machine does not reside inside the sentences. The syntax, rhythm, imagery, and argument are the same; the emotional effect may be the same. Whatever extra value comes from knowing who wrote one of them is not linguistic value. It is provenance, and provenance can matter enormously.

A letter from a dead father, a prison diary, a declaration of love — there the author’s biography is part of the product. Most language does not work that way.

Ask why inflation rose and you want the best explanation. Ask how to fix a router and you want instructions that work. The identity of the producer may be part of the pleasure, but it is not necessarily relevant.

The confusion is fusing two different propositions into one.

Two Propositions

The first claim is that human-written language possesses qualities machine-generated language cannot reproduce — an empirical claim about output, which weakens as models improve. The second is that people sometimes care that a human wrote something — a preference about provenance, which can remain valid forever. The second is not evidence for the first.

A reader can prefer a human novelist simply because they enjoy knowing a particular person imagined the book. That is not irrational; it is consuming the existence of the novelist along with the novel.

AI is forcing a separation of things that were historically bundled.

A beautiful paragraph once implied a capable writer, a brilliant argument an intelligent thinker, a funny line a funny person. The artifact carried two kinds of information at once: about itself, and about its creator. Generative AI breaks the connection — the artifact survives while the inference about the producer disappears.

The Fallible Witness

The same break appears in our relationship with truth. Reputation gets treated as a truth-enforcement mechanism: this person is respected, so they would have too much to lose by lying.

Lying is a calculation, not a character test.

The relevant question is whether the expected benefit of the deception exceeds the expected cost. People exaggerate, omit, misremember, rationalize, flatter themselves, and conform. A person can be completely sincere and still be wrong, which is why sincerity and reliability are not the same thing. Human communication serves many goals at once; truth is one objective among many.

Imagine a system with 99.9999 percent accuracy on a particular class of questions.

At that point the relevant question is no longer whether the machine is human but whether it is more reliable.

Language models are fallible today; they fabricate, make mistakes, and express uncertainty poorly. There is no reason to assume this unreliability is permanent. Systems can be built that retrieve evidence directly, expose uncertainty, and refuse to invent missing details.

Ask a person whether they sent the contract on Tuesday and you may get a sincere, confident, wrong answer. A sufficiently capable system can inspect the record and answer with timestamps.

More authentic, not more accurate.

The hierarchy may invert: verified machine output, then trusted human testimony, then unverified machine output, then untrusted human testimony. Not because machines are morally superior, but because they may be better calibrated. Humans keep one authority — their own inner states: whether you forgive me, what you want, whether something hurt you.

The Separated Rewards

The resistance is not only about truth. Language production has never been only a transfer of value to the audience; it has also been how the producer acquires value. A writer gains recognition, a journalist credibility, a comedian admiration, a professor authority. When a person says something insightful at dinner, the people around them begin to regard that person as intelligent.

Those two transactions normally align.

The reader still receives the excellent story, the student the excellent explanation, the audience the joke. But the person who previously gained money, fame, and identity from producing that value may no longer be necessary. If AI remained permanently mediocre, the argument would largely disappear; humans could point to the inferior output and keep their role. The genuinely threatening scenario is the machine becoming better.

When readers prefer machine novels and students learn faster from machine tutors, the human producer cannot say you need me because I provide the better experience. The argument becomes you should prefer me because I am human.

That is no longer an argument about the consumer’s welfare; it is an argument about preserving the social value of the producer.

People already make this tradeoff. They pay more for handmade objects when machine-made ones work as well; they celebrate craftsmanship and live performance. Sometimes the provenance is the product, and then there is no contradiction — but when the buyer does not care who made it, asking them to choose the inferior object to protect the maker’s status is a different kind of demand.

A great deal of human status has depended on scarcity.

Few people could write a great novel, explain quantum mechanics clearly, or make thousands of people laugh. The scarcity of those abilities made the people who had them valuable. When technology destroys scarcity, the people who depended on it begin emphasizing the qualities that supposedly cannot be automated.

Photography made painting justify itself as more than accurate reproduction.

Recorded music changed the meaning of live performance, and computers forced chess players to admit that human excellence was not the highest possible level of chess. Generative AI is doing the same to language — it is separating the act of creating from the credit for creating.

The pot holds water either way.

From the consumer’s side this is abundance; from the displaced producer’s side, dispossession. Both are true. The real argument over AI was never whether machines can create meaningful language; it is what happens to a society in which useful things no longer require useful people to create them.

The deepest discomfort of artificial intelligence is not that machines are learning to write. It is that people are being forced to see how much of what they called the value of the pot was actually the value they hoped the pot would confer on the potter.
