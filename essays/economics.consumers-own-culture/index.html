Consumers Own Culture

Why cultural industries do not control their economy — the consumer is the final boss and moralizing against preference is fighting gravity

  • economics
  • culture
  • consumer-sovereignty
  • attention
  • factor-mobility
  • hollywood

The crowbars go in on a Tuesday, after three weeks of filming. The 1920s brownstone on the backlot has fire escapes that open onto nothing and a front door that locks with a key, and the crew that built it out of cut timber, vacuum-molded plastic, and sculpted polystyrene tears it down board by board and feeds it to a dumpster marked landfill.

That is the cultural economy in one image: rainforest timber and hired crews spent on something destroyed on purpose, while the people who did the building are told to be grateful for the privilege.

The art world, publishing, and film share one delusion: that they are the custodians of culture and can dictate what the public should value, and when tastes shift they call it a decline in culture rather than a shift in preference. In 1995 the studios had a captive audience; if you wanted video entertainment, you bought a ticket or watched three networks. Today the menu is infinite — TikTok, games, YouTube, Substack, a book, or a walk outside — and cinema, the first art born of the Industrial Revolution, still treats its medium as a permanent fixture instead of a capital-intensive way to capture attention at a particular moment in technological history.

The industry did not lose its monopoly on distribution; it lost its monopoly on attention.

The Art Market Decides

Consumers can do whatever they want. They may decide to read books, or they may decide not to think about entertainment at all; they may decide they no longer want to own cars and would rather ride bicycles, or tricycles, or take a train. None of it requires the industry’s permission, because money is only a claim on other people’s labor and resources, and value is established by the mutual consent of buyer and seller — no union contract or antitrust suit changes the consumer’s desire.

The only honest anchor in business is relentless consumer obsession; build your model around what the customer actually wants and you stop spending energy fighting gravity, you flow where the water flows. The consumer is the final boss, and moralizing against the fact is fighting gravity.

There is nothing content gives you that you could not do without it.

Content Is Time, Not Treasure

A computer is a capital good, a tool you use to produce other things; a movie, a book, or a video game is a consumption good, a way to spend an hour the day will never refund. Content reproduces at zero marginal cost but runs on one scarce input, the human attention span, and a day holds only twenty-four hours. If a teenager gets more satisfaction from a fifteen-second TikTok than from a three-hour movie, the TikTok is economically superior — more utility per unit of time and cost. Hollywood believes spending $200 million makes a movie inherently valuable; divide the money by the three hours and the hour costs roughly sixty-seven million dollars, and the teenager’s hour owes nothing to the industry’s budget.

The Set That Could Have Been a House

The set is the arithmetic. The carpenters who build a temporary brownstone out of MDF can build permanent housing, and the welders, electricians, and riggers can build transit and power grids; soundstages are warehouses with high ceilings, and the generators and trucks get auctioned to construction sites and logistics firms. The lumber that framed a fake brownstone was rainforest timber, and the same boards could frame an apartment wall. Catherine the Great toured the Crimea in 1787, and the villages along her route were painted façades erected by her favorite, Potemkin — purpose-built scenery for an audience of one, dismantled after she passed.

When labor and capital move from a destroy-after-use industry to a built-to-last one, the net productivity of the whole economy rises; a shrinking Hollywood is not a tragedy, it is a release of raw materials and skilled labor back into the economy, to build things people actually need. Hollywood is not a small author’s room; it is a heavy-industrial construction project disguised as art, and a whole city of soundstages, lumber yards, catering, and hotels was built around it. When the production contracts, the truck drivers, the electricians, and the local small businesses see their revenue dry up, which is why the industry gets treated like steel or autos instead of like a creative field.

Policy debates count only what is seen: the jobs that stay, the studio that survives. They do not count what is never seen: the houses never built, the startups never funded, the value never created.

The panic is not about the carpenters.

Why the Lobby Is Loud

The people who lobby hardest are the hyper-specialists whose skills mean nothing outside a movie set: the script supervisor, the color-grader, the camera focus-puller who cannot focus-pull a construction site. Their human capital is a sunk asset tied to one declining industry, and their personal value evaporates outside the container they defend, so they moralize; they call a shift in preference a decline in culture, because the alternative is admitting they bet a career on the wrong technology. The panic is loudest where the transition is hardest, which is why Hollywood gets treated like an untouchable cultural monument that society is obligated to subsidize. The monument is a set, and the set is already scheduled for demolition.

You cannot legislate consumer attention.

The set comes down either way. The timber and the crews go on to build something that lasts, and no complaint about culture will make the consumer watch what they have stopped watching.
