Executive Pay, Profits, and Worker Pay

CEO pay spread across a workforce is a few hundred dollars a year per worker — the pool that could actually move wages is profits, and the two are never the same act

  • economics
  • executive-pay
  • wages
  • inequality
  • profits

Pour a glass of water into a reservoir and the waterline barely moves. Spread a chief executive’s paycheck across the workforce of a large company and each worker receives a splash.

The slogan says profits are being hoarded by executives. The arithmetic says otherwise, but only for one version of the claim. Reallocating a CEO’s compensation, reallocating a company’s annual profit, and liquidating its retained earnings are three different operations with three different magnitudes. The debate treats them as one act, which is how a careful policy question became a bumper sticker.

The three are not the same act.

The Glass

The point is not that CEO pay is small in absolute terms. The median S&P 500 package ran about $16.5 million in 2024, and the Russell 3000 median about $6.7 million. Those are life-changing numbers for one household, and a rounding error across a payroll.

Recent proxy filings make the size of the glass plain. Walmart’s fiscal 2025 CEO compensation was about $27.4 million; Microsoft’s, about $96.5 million. Most of each package is stock, not salary — Walmart’s included about $20.4 million of stock awards, Microsoft’s about $84.2 million.

Spread across Walmart’s workforce of 2.1 million people, that is roughly $13 per worker. At Microsoft, roughly $423.

Add the disclosed top executive pools and the per-worker numbers still stay small: about $48 at Walmart, $852 at Microsoft, and $423 at JPMorgan. The sector spread tells the rest of the story: Walmart runs on roughly $9,255 of profit per employee, Microsoft on roughly $446,632.

The glass is small; the splash per worker is a few hundred dollars at best.

The Reservoir

The reservoir is the pool that can actually move the waterline. If a firm redistributed its entire annual profit to workers equally, the gains stop being rounding error. At a 100-worker firm, equal distribution works out to roughly $5,700 per worker a year at a 2 percent margin, $22,900 at 8 percent, and $57,200 at 20 percent. At a 5,000-worker firm the same math gives $10,700, $42,600, and $106,600; at 50,000 workers, $8,400, $33,700, and $84,200.

That is no longer rounding error.

The same ledger shows up at the macro scale. In the BEA’s 2025 national accounts for corporate business, compensation of employees was $9.905 trillion, after-tax profits $2.856 trillion, net dividends $1.707 trillion, and undistributed profits $1.149 trillion. Labor compensation dwarfs profit; profit then splits between what is paid out and what is kept. Executive pay does not register on that scale at all.

Shareholder distributions make the same point at the firm level: Microsoft paid $42.5 billion in dividends and buybacks in fiscal 2025 against $96.5 million in CEO compensation, and ExxonMobil returned $37.2 billion. The pools that dwarf the CEO are dividends, buybacks, and retained earnings — capital markets, not payroll decisions.

Compare those gains to existing pay levels. Reallocating the CEO package equals about 18 percent of average payroll in the 100-worker case, 1.7 percent in the 5,000-worker case, and 0.5 percent in the 50,000-worker case. Reallocating all profits at the 8 percent margin equals about 41 percent, 53 percent, and 47 percent of payroll. The answer to what changes worker earnings materially is profit-sharing rules, not CEO pay caps.

The water that could irrigate the field is the inflow, not the glass.

The Dam

The reservoir has a dam, and the dam is the part people misunderstand. Retained earnings are a stock built up over years, not cash sitting in a vault. Microsoft reported $237.7 billion in retained earnings at June 30, 2025, against $94.6 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments. Walmart’s retained earnings at January 31, 2025 were $98.3 billion.

“Give the workers the retained earnings” is not a recurring wage policy; it is a one-time recapitalization that would require cash, borrowing, or asset sales. Drain the reservoir to water the fields and there is no reservoir left to keep the fields alive next season.

The other complication is which way the water flows. When firms distribute profits through dividends, buybacks, and rising share prices, the top 1 percent of households holds 50.2 percent of corporate equities and mutual fund shares, the top decile about 87.4 percent, and the bottom half 1.1 percent. Households are inside the system too — U.S. retirement assets reached $49.1 trillion at year-end 2025 — but exposure is not equality.

Broad participation, highly unequal amounts.

The serious discussion is about surplus-sharing institutions: recurring profit-sharing, broad equity grants, employee ownership, bargaining over surplus. Those mechanisms are connected to worker pay in a way that shaving a few executive packages is not. Executive-pay reform may matter for governance and fairness; as mass-income policy it is the wrong lever.

None of this makes the anger wrong. The pool is real, and a company that shares it can move worker pay by thousands of dollars a year. Chopping the CEO’s salary is pouring the glass back into the reservoir and calling the waterline movement progress. Share the inflow and the fields drink.
