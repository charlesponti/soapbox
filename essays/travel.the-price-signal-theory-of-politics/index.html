The Price Signal Theory of Politics

Why abolishing prices abolishes information, and who profits from the blindness

  • prices
  • mao
  • famine
  • central-planning
  • socialism
  • economics

A house at night, and the wall thermostat does its one job: a bimetal coil that bends as the room cools, tilting a mercury switch until the circuit closes and the furnace in the basement fires. The metal does not set the temperature. It reports it. The house obeys the report.

Prices are the thermostat of a society: they do not decide who deserves grain, they report where grain is running out, and the furnace of a whole economy obeys the report. Smash the thermostat and the furnace keeps burning — it just burns blind.

Mao’s Great Leap Forward is the loudest example of what happens to a system that cannot read scarcity: from 1958 to 1962, China’s regime replaced market prices with centralized quotas and ideological decree. The result is called the Great Famine, and it is not a story about weather. It is a story about information.

The Famine the Thermostat Could Have Seen

The ideology assumed abundance was the natural state; nature and human productivity were effectively limitless, and the only reason anyone was poor or hungry was that bad actors — landlords, hoarders, saboteurs — were holding supply back out of malice, a hoard hidden in the very ground. When shortages came, the planners did not read them as physical facts about land, weather, and labor, the only facts that could have helped. To the planner, a starving village was not evidence of a bad harvest. It was evidence of a conspiracy.

So the requisitions began. Grain was taken from villages that were already starving, down to the seed grain, because the central planner had convinced himself the grain must exist and the farmers were hiding it, and the hiding place must be the village itself. Local officials, facing impossible party targets, reported record harvests that never happened, and the central government believed the reports, exported grain abroad to demonstrate communist superiority, and its own rural population starved. Exporting grain while people starve is the signature of a system that has lost its eyes. The official harvest numbers climbed while the real ones fell, because the two had stopped being connected to the same world.

Meanwhile the peasants, paid the same no matter what they grew, stopped producing; private land ownership had been abolished, output collapsed because nothing rewarded it, and the commune paid the industrious and the idle alike, so the industrious became idle, the incentive vacuum pulling every field toward nothing. The backyard furnaces that melted cooking pots to hit a steel quota did not make steel; they made the point that a quota is not a signal, that a number without a market is just a wish. The furnace kept burning on the report, and the report was a lie.

A gauge that lies is worse than no gauge, because the house keeps trusting it and the furnace keeps firing on the false report. There was no price, only a target, and the target lied to save itself.

A price for grain would have screamed the truth into the ledger.

Producers Know When Scarcity Is Real

The core belief that produces all of this — that abundance is being withheld by saboteurs — dies on one question: if a producer actually possessed infinite supply, what would they do with it, hoard it for the pleasure of owning a mountain of corn? They would flood the market, scale endlessly, and capture total volume, because restricting supply restricts their own wealth too. Restricting supply only makes sense when the asset is finite and costly to make. No producer restrains infinite abundance; the entire idea is a contradiction dressed as a moral claim.

The pattern is older than the century. Diocletian fixed the price of every good in the empire with a single edict in the year 301, and the goods simply left the markets, because no producer will sell at a price that reports nothing true.

Scarcity is a law of physics, not a moral failure, and the regime could not tell the difference. If the producers are the people who would know whether abundance is possible, and their dominant move at infinite abundance is to produce more, then a permanent shortage is proof that the ideology is wrong, not that the hoarders are winning. The whole belief system rests on the idea that the people who produce everything are conspiring to produce less. That is not an economic theory; it is a witch hunt.

The Coordinator Class

So central planning survives its own famines, because the people who run it are not the people who starve, and the memory of hunger never makes it to the committee room. The coordinator class — academics, bureaucrats, planners, media elites — does not picture itself in the breadline when it argues for state allocation; it pictures itself on the planning committee, at the long table, with the charts. Under a market, a bureaucrat has zero control over who gets what; a million anonymous buyers decide, while under central planning, allocation is decided by experts and administrators, which is to say by them.

The push for state control is not an act of solidarity with the bottom; it is the most effective form of gatekeeping ever designed, concentrating the power to decide who eats into the hands of the people arguing that nobody should have to worry about eating. This is also why the working class tends to support markets: a dollar is blind, and a $20 bill from a laborer buys the same goods as a $20 bill from a politician, while under state allocation, resources are distributed by political currency — who you know, your standing with the party, your ability to navigate the bureaucracy — and the laborer has none of it.

For a working person with no political power, the market is the only leverage they have.

Removing prices does not remove the gatekeepers; it replaces a system where consumers decide who succeeds with a system where the same handful of people decide who eats. The persistence is structural: higher education trains every new generation of thinkers, and central planning is the one system that crowns them rightful leaders, while market capitalism demotes the intellectual — a plumber who builds something people want outearns a philosopher — so the blindness is not an accident; it is a business model for the coordinator class, and it keeps reinvesting in itself. Every generation reaches adulthood, sees the real flaws of the market — housing, health care, inequality — and the promise of a top-down fix feels new again, because no one has watched one fail recently.

The thermostat was smashed, the house burned, and the people who smashed it found the warmth useful.
