The Laws Don't Care

Economic law is as indifferent to political intention as gravity is to opinion — policy changes who administers scarcity, not whether the water moves

  • economics
  • scarcity
  • policy
  • arbitrage
  • capitalism

Tariffs were supposed to bring the jobs home. In 2021 the United States imposed the largest expansion of tariffs on Chinese goods in a generation, and three years later the jobs had not come back — automation had.

The math had routed around the policy the way water routes around a rock.

Faced with a legal barrier to cheap foreign labor, manufacturers did what the law of substitution predicts: they shifted capital from human workers to machines. The tariff did not restore labor leverage; it accelerated labor’s replacement. The rock did not stop the water. It changed which path the water took.

The rock is always there. The only variable is how long the water takes.

The Definition Nobody Quotes

Economics is the study of how humans allocate scarce resources that have alternative uses. The definition contains everything and almost never appears in political debate, because it makes the project of economic policy sound constrained. If a worker’s time can be sold in Los Angeles or Bucharest, or replaced by software, the price of that time is set by competition between those alternatives, not by the worker’s preference or the employer’s sympathy. If a government subsidizes housing, the money comes from somewhere and is not available somewhere else.

None of this is a feature of capitalism. It is a feature of scarcity. Change the system and you change who makes the allocation decisions, not the fact that every choice forecloses another.

The resources do not multiply because the administrator has better intentions.

The Soviet Union discovered this, and so did Venezuela. Price a thing below what it costs to make, and less of it gets made; the shelves empty not because the state was corrupt but because the law of supply and demand has no exception for good intentions. This is not ideology. It is arithmetic applied to human behavior.

The Three Laws

The law of arbitrage is the most persistent of them. Arbitrage is exploiting a price difference between markets, and as long as two markets with different costs can be connected, the expensive one faces pressure.

The connection only has to be possible.

When American manufacturing moved to China in the 1990s and 2000s, the firms were not expressing a political preference. They were answering a labor-cost difference between two markets that global supply chains had just connected. Policy can make the connection more expensive. It cannot eliminate the cost difference, so the law of substitution reroutes the search: hire fewer humans, automate, or relocate.

The water finds the path. The rock changes which path it takes, not whether water moves.

The law of opportunity cost is the one that makes every promise of a free lunch worth examining. There is no free lunch; the cost of any choice is the best alternative given up. A housing subsidy can be funded by taxing production, printing money, or borrowing, and each path has a price that does not disappear when the path obscures it.

A Nordic social democracy does not eliminate opportunity cost. It makes different choices about where the cost falls, and those may be better choices — still choices with costs.

Tariffs were one attempt to close the connection. Import restrictions, local-content rules, capital controls, and subsidies to domestic industry each raise the price of the connection. Each one meets the same substitution: the firm automates, relocates, or reshapes the product until the barrier no longer binds.

The law of elasticity is the most human. Every person has a threshold at which the cost of compliance exceeds the benefit, and they change their behavior to survive. Below subsistence wages, people stop showing up; above affordable prices, people find substitutes; past tolerable taxes, people restructure. The Soviet command economy did not repeal it — it produced a vast informal economy that routed around official prices.

Every system finds the wall.

The Enemy Is a Mechanism

This is what makes the anger of modern economic discontent so hard to dismiss. The jobs that went away did not come back. The wages that stagnated did not keep pace with housing.

The explanation that the suffering was caused by a system, and can be cured by changing the system, is not quite right. The suffering came from immutable laws meeting specific history: global labor markets opening, knowledge work digitizing, cheap credit running for a decade. A different system would have administered that history differently. It would not have made the history different.

A worker in Los Angeles competing with a worker in Bucharest is not competing against capitalism. She is competing against a wage differential that can close only if one side gets more expensive, the other cheaper, or the connection becomes impossible.

The politician who promises otherwise is not lying. They are assigning a human face to an impersonal force, creating an enemy where there is only a mechanism, and selling the belief that the right administrator could make the water stop flowing. The water does not have a political preference; it finds the lowest available point.

Knowing how the mechanism works is the precondition for working with it. A doctor who understands physiology does not conclude that medicine is useless; she concludes that interventions must respect the mechanism they redirect.

The debate worth having is not capitalism versus socialism.

It is the narrower question of which path to make available, given that the water will always find one. That question has answers.

It just does not have villains, and a debate without villains is a very hard thing to run a campaign on.
