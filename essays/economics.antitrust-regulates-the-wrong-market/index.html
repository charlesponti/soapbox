The Dam on the Rerouted River

Antitrust regulators police the distribution pipe while consumers spend attention — a lawsuit over a river that already rerouted

  • antitrust
  • media
  • attention-economy
  • hollywood
  • regulation

Nine at night, and the living room has split itself across three screens: a phone in one hand, a tablet propped on a knee, a television on the wall running a show nobody picked. That morning in Sacramento, the state’s lawyers filed a complaint about a $110 billion merger, and they drew the box around it — theatrical motion picture distribution and linear basic cable. The box contains seats nobody is sitting in.

A river that reroutes leaves its paperwork behind. The permit office still certifies the old channel as the river, because that is where the permits were written; the water has been a mile away for a decade, and it cut its own bed through the gravel on the way. Antitrust law defines markets by the distribution pipe — the theater screen, the cable bundle, the linear schedule. Consumers spend attention, not distribution, and the two stopped being the same market years ago.

The fight is over a channel that no longer carries the water.

The Market the Law Sees

Inside the box the state drew, the merger looks enormous. Paramount and Warner Bros. control roughly a third of theatrical films and basic cable channels between them, and under Section 7 of the Clayton Act the case lives or dies on the relevant product market. Draw the box narrow and the combined company is a monopoly; draw it wide and it is a medium-sized player in a crowded room. The law keeps redrawing the old box.

The box describes a world that stopped existing.

The Market That Exists

People have roughly sixteen waking hours a day, and every one of them competes. Two hours at a cinema, two hours on Netflix, an hour on TikTok, an hour on Twitch, a scroll through Instagram, a game on a phone — the fixed pie is attention, and time is the finite currency. Paramount and Warner Bros. do not compete with each other first; they compete with YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Twitch, and mobile gaming for a slice of the same sixteen hours. Raise the ticket price or ship a bad film and the consumer is not trapped; the substitute is one tap away, which is why the elasticity is high.

The clearest casualty of the reroute is the mid-budget theatrical film. The middle collapsed because audiences decided they do not leave the house for average films — they stream those at home and reserve the theater for the massive, the visual, the immersive. Studios cut their slates not out of a conspiracy to starve supply but because the market will no longer profitably absorb the middle; the fewer movies are a response to demand, not a cause of it.

Watch the arithmetic of the new channel. A twenty-year-old who built a following with YouTube shorts directed a feature for A24 on a ten-million-dollar budget, and it opened to more than eighty million in a weekend — eight dollars back for every one spent. A micro-budget horror made for under a million crossed a hundred million. A creator self-financed his film on three million and pushed it into thousands of theaters. None of that ran through a studio slate, a cable bundle, or a permit.

The market the law protects is not the market that pays for the movies.

Why the State Guards the Dry Channel

Courts are bound by stare decisis, and the relevant precedent is Brown Shoe from 1962 and the Paramount Pictures case from 1948. No fifty-year-old Supreme Court opinion defines TikTok substitutability, so a state attorney general cannot easily stand before a federal judge and argue the attention economy; it is far easier to cite the old cases that treat movie theaters as a standalone market. Legal precedent moves slowly, and the definition was locked before most of the current audience was born.

The defense of the old box leans on the SSNIP test: if a firm raises prices five to ten percent, would enough customers switch to make it unprofitable? Regulators answer no, because someone who wants a two-hour cinematic, narrative experience will not accept a fifteen-second clip in that moment. The test measures the switch that happens this quarter; the market moved over a decade, and the test cannot see a decade.

The incentives finish the job. Enforcement agencies win budgets by showing they police big business, so a high-profile suit against a major brand produces headlines and political capital. A state attorney general can spend ten million dollars in legal fees to delay a merger and lose, and the cost lands on taxpayers while nobody faces a career penalty for a losing theory. Time is a weapon, and regulators wield it openly: drawn-out litigation forces deal fatigue, concessions, and consent decrees that bind the merged company to keep making a certain number of theatrical films.

Local interests push the same direction. Theater chains need a steady pipeline of theatrical-exclusive releases; cable operators are losing subscribers to cord-cutting; guilds bargain against consolidation. The state defends obsolete economics anyway, because every incentive points there and the economics are beside the point.

The Los Angeles River holds the same certificate: the Army Corps paved it into a concrete flood-control ditch, the water was rerouted and managed, and the maps still call the concrete a river. Designation outlives water; that is what designation is for.

The state will spend months and millions holding a dam across a channel the water left behind. The river does not need the dam’s permission; it moved a mile away, and it is still moving.
