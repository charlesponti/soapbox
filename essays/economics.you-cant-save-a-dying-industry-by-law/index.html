You Can't Save a Dying Industry by Law

Why a union lawsuit that blocks a studio merger speeds the bankruptcy, liquidation, and offshoring it claims to prevent

  • economics
  • antitrust
  • labor-unions
  • hollywood
  • media
  • creative-destruction

The diesel idles at the platform, and the man beside it holds a shovel. He held it when the boilers burned coal, he held it when the fireboxes switched to oil, and he holds it now that the coal car sits empty and the union has made the railroad keep paying for the shovel. The passengers boarding do not see him. The engine does not need him. The railroad pays anyway, because losing the argument costs less than losing the lawsuit.

The Writers Guild is suing to keep a man shoveling coal into a locomotive that runs on oil. The railroads switched to diesel decades ago, the coal is gone, and the union’s answer is to make the railroad keep paying the man anyway. In Hollywood the coal shovel is the $110 billion merger between Paramount and Warner Brothers Discovery, which the WGA and twelve state attorneys general have filed antitrust suits to block.

The lawsuit reads as a defense of competition. What it defends is a structure that no longer clears its own bills. Studios do not merge because executives are greedy or stupid; they merge because the ground is collapsing beneath them, and consolidation is the one move that keeps the lights on a few years longer. Shareholders and executives do not want to merge; they want the company to survive, and merging is the best way to protect its long-term interest rather than wait things out and go out of business.

Block the merger and the alternative is not five healthy studios. The alternative is bankruptcy, IP liquidation to tech monopolies, and runaway production. The union’s victory is the mechanism of the union’s own defeat.

The Feathered Fireman

For nearly a century, steam locomotives required a fireman to shovel coal into the boiler alongside the engineer. When diesel engines arrived in the 1930s and 1940s, there was literally no coal left to shovel, and the rail unions dug in. They argued the fireman was still essential as a second pair of eyes on the gauges, a safety man for the run. Economists named it featherbedding: paying a man for a job a fuel line had already completed.

The railroads called it being forced to carry hundreds of millions of dollars in unnecessary labor costs at the exact moment they needed capital to upgrade track and compete with the new interstate highways. The fight dragged on for decades, through strikes, national security seizures, and presidential commissions. Railroads were legally forced to keep hiring firemen on diesel trains well into the 1960s. The featherbedding, combined with federal rate regulation, pushed multiple major railroads into bankruptcy by the 1970s. In the end the government had to deregulate and allow massive mergers to keep the national network from collapsing.

The typographers ran the same script. Cold type replaced the Linotype machines, the International Typographical Union struck New York’s dailies for 114 days, and in 1974 The New York Times bought peace with lifetime employment guarantees. The composing rooms emptied by attrition, and the union itself dissolved.

Both unions won their fight and lost their industry.

The Arithmetic of Bankruptcy

Run the same fight forward in Hollywood and the outcome is arithmetic. A studio barred from merging does not keep spending on original films; it runs out of cash and buries itself in debt. Bankruptcy is not a writer-room layoff. It is truck drivers, set builders, VFX artists, and administrators out of work, and a library auctioned to companies that want content to feed hardware, not movies to play in theaters. A consolidated studio that makes thirty films a year is still infinitely better for workers than a bankrupt studio that makes zero.

If the studio goes under, the library gets bought up by even larger tech monopolies that do not care about the theatrical business and just want content to feed their hardware ecosystems. Apple, Amazon, and Google pay pennies for catalogues that once anchored a studio’s valuation. They do not keep writer rooms running. They do not keep Los Angeles employed.

The second consequence is geographic, and it is already running. The WGA holds jurisdiction only over productions made in the United States, and entertainment is a global commodity; if the cost of producing a show in Los Angeles becomes economically unviable, studios do not stop making content — they move the production to the UK, Canada, Australia, or Eastern Europe, where governments offer massive tax incentives and crews cost less. A debt-strapped independent studio has no choice but to shoot its next series in Budapest.

By forcing independent, debt-strapped studios to remain separate and maintain high-cost structures, the unions accelerate the incentive to outsource. A consolidated, healthy studio has the capital to keep some high-end production in Los Angeles. A struggling, independent studio facing bankruptcy cannot. Every rule that raises the cost of American production is a rule that exports American jobs, and the fireman’s shovel leans unused against the cab.

Sympathy does not balance a balance sheet.

Why the Union Fights Anyway

The puzzle is why the WGA sues when the math is this plain. A union’s mandate is narrow: protect the immediate wages, working conditions, and job security of current members. The executive’s job is to keep the company solvent; the union’s job is to stop the surviving entity from squeezing the workers who remain. One side watches the forest, the other watches the trees. The consumer attention and revenue that funded those studios are evaporating, and no lawsuit changes that. Neither side can force the consumer back into the theater.

The loudest voices belong to the people who can least afford the transition. The carpenters who build sets can build houses anywhere; their skills travel. The hyper-specialists cannot: the script supervisor, the color-grader, the camera focus-puller whose skill means nothing outside a movie set, whose human capital evaporates the day the industry does. They are the firemen of this era, and they lobby hardest for the shovel.

Every year the railroad keeps paying them, their position gets worse.

The diesel will not be converted back to coal because a court says so. The men who sued to keep shoveling will be the last ones off the train, paid to do nothing until the railroad dies anyway.
