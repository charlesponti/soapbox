The House We Refuse to Build

Why billionaires fund libraries but never housing, and why by-right zoning is the only lever that clears the channel

  • economics
  • housing
  • zoning
  • philanthropy
  • by-right
  • yimby

On a Tuesday afternoon, in the boardroom of a large foundation, a staffer slides a one-page memo across the table. Requested: two hundred million dollars, for a new wing of the city museum — glass, cantilever, an architect’s name in the papers. The trustees approve in eleven minutes. The same foundation has never been asked to build a hundred apartments, and the staffer knows better than to ask. A wing is a ceremony with a plaque. A hundred units is a maintenance schedule, a rent roll, and a tenant who will end up in the news.

Housing is the everything problem, the substrate the whole economy sits on. When a household hands over 35, 45, or 50 percent of its income for shelter, that money stops feeding the restaurants, the schools, the next venture — every dollar trapped in rent is a dollar that cannot start a business or buy a future. The river still wants to run to the sea, and the builders are the current. They say they could raise a hundred good houses a day if the channel were clear. A century of stacked regulations has silted it shut, and the water barely moves.

The damage compounds downstream. Workers cannot move to the cities with the jobs, labor stops matching talent to work, and young people defer the milestones that turn renters into taxpayers and families.

Shelter is the tax on everything, paid monthly, forever.

The Wing and the Water

A housing project carries slumlord risk, and the donor knows it. The man who writes the check for the museum wing walks away clean — the plaque is installed, the gala is photographed, the name is set in brass. The donor who funds a hundred units is now in the property business: rents to collect, roofs to fix, evictions to handle, tenants to satisfy. One disrepair story and the legacy is a headline. Rockefeller and Carnegie could buy land and build model towns with no local oversight; their heirs watched Pullman turn into a strike and public housing into a scandal, and learned the lesson. Don’t touch the roof.

The builders feel the same choke from below. Every month a lot sits idle waiting on discretionary review, and the carrying costs compound — interest on the land loan, site maintenance, overhead — each idle month one more inch of silt. These are not speculators; they are the crews who can frame a foundation-to-roof house in under two months, built to code, inspected, safe.

By the time the green light arrives, the cost basis has exploded, and the only project that still pencils is luxury. The system does not merely permit expensive housing — it outlaws everything else by making the timeline so long that only high-margin units survive.

The Vote in the Room

The objections that justify the choke come from the town hall, and the town hall is a structurally corrupt sample. The people in the room are the present residents, free on a Tuesday night, motivated by fear of change. The people missing are the nurse who would move into the new building in two years, the young family priced out last month, the worker commuting ninety minutes from outside the city. Asking current neighbors whether new housing should be built is like asking the diners in a crowded restaurant whether to lock the front door. The sample is the flaw, not the people in it.

Of course they vote to lock it.

Their feelings are real, and they are not relevant — no more than your taste for techno should let you ban every other genre in Los Angeles. A preference you can enforce at zero cost becomes a tax on people who are not in the room.

The infrastructure excuse is the same veto in a different costume. Opponents who cannot stop a building by height sue under environmental law, claiming the sewer cannot handle the load or that traffic will rise four percent, and the city spends three to five years on hydrological studies. Not because the engineering is hard, but because concern is cheaper than consent.

Dredge, Don’t Debate

Minneapolis ended single-family-only zoning in 2018. While its neighbors saw double-digit rent growth, Minneapolis rents grew about one percent over several years. Studies put the city’s home prices and rents sixteen to thirty-four percent lower than they would have been without the reforms. One city legalized the duplex; the whole region’s ledger moved.

Houston cut its minimum lot size from 5,000 to 1,400 square feet and got 80,000 townhomes, unsubsidized. California made garage conversions by-right, and ADU production went from about 1,000 a year to over 20,000 — one in five new units in the state. Montana required duplexes by-right statewide in 2023, Washington followed, and the pattern repeats wherever the channel is cleared.

The stacking effect is why single fixes fail. Legalize the duplex and ten other rules still stand — parking minimums, lot coverage caps, historic overlays — each one enough to kill the project alone, so clearing one rule leaves the channel silted.

The same lever answers the luxury objection. Today’s luxury is tomorrow’s affordable housing — every cheap flat you can rent was once new and expensive. Block the $1.5 million condo and the wealthy buyers do not leave the city; they buy the older stock, renovate it, and the displacement happens anyway, slower, crueler, uncredited.

Preservation is an identity project, not a policy. It lets a person feel like the curator of the city while the artists they claim to love are priced onto the highway — the cheapest form of heroism a person can buy.

The West Village kept its bricks and lost its people — one hundred percent of the facades, zero percent of the culture.

The dredge has to come from above the waterline. State and national preemption strips the local veto entirely — a city that cannot ban duplexes cannot kill the project with a hearing. This is how the country built the Erie Canal: the state did not ask each town along the ditch whether the water might pass; it dug, and the money followed the channel. By-right is the dredge — take the discretionary veto out of the water, and the river clears itself.

The channel is silted, and the current has not weakened. No argument at a town hall will move the mud, because the people holding the shovel are not the ones who piled it there.

The river has been waiting a century to run.
