The Two Curators

Both camps preserve and both disrupt, and the real argument is over which wings stay open

  • politics
  • conservatism
  • progressivism
  • institutions
  • preservation

Walk into the museum on a quiet Tuesday and the argument is already in progress. A docent leads a school group past a wing that is taped off, and the tape is new. Upstairs, a sign is coming down and another is going up, and the name over the entrance is the same name it was last year, which is exactly what the committee is fighting about in the back office. Nobody is carrying a sledgehammer. Everyone is carrying a key.

Nobody on either side wants to tear the building down; that would be madness, and the roof over both of them would be gone with it, along with every argument they are having inside it. They fight instead about which wings stay open, which collections get funding, which doors get locked, which signs come down, and which name gets hung over the entrance when the committee changes hands. The building is safe. The argument is over the inventory, and the inventory is the only thing that was ever worth arguing about.

The most progressive party in politics is also its most conservative.

The Sign on the Door

Consider what the modern left actually defends. The historic character of a single-family neighborhood, defended against the six-story building that would change the street and the parking and the light and the market value of the whole block. Old-growth forests, wetlands, and the precise configuration of an ecosystem, defended against the industrial progress that would flatten them. The civil service, the unions, the safety net, the norms of the institution, the guardrails built over a century, all defended against deregulation, privatization, and disruption. None of this is radical; it is stewardship, and it is the most traditional impulse in the room, the impulse that built every historic district the party now protects.

It rejects corporate innovation on principle, and not because the new is dangerous, and not because the old is sacred. The gig platform, the AI that replaces labor, the redevelopment that erases a neighborhood — these are refused because they are market-driven, and the market is the wing this party trusts least.

Meanwhile the modern right stopped being the party of preservation some time ago. The old conservatism defended the civil service, the corporate board, the intelligence agency, the elite university, the whole load-bearing skeleton of establishment life, the stable door and the man who keeps it; the new conservatism looks at the same institutions and sees entrenched power that needs dismantling. It is now the party that wants to break the building’s rules, not maintain them.

School vouchers exist to break the government’s monopoly on education; deregulation exists to let new players into energy, finance, and technology; the anti-institutional reflex treats every established hierarchy as a suspect, every gatekeeper as a toll collector. This is libertarianism wearing a party pin, closer in spirit to a startup founder than to a squire, and it has more in common with the disruptors than with the last century of its own party.

Both parties are now in the demolition business; they just pick different buildings.

What Each One Keeps

So the axis that still works is not progress against tradition; it is what each side wants to preserve. The left preserves ecosystems, institutional safety nets, and the local community’s architecture, and disrupts markets, corporations, and whatever threatens those; the right preserves individual autonomy, property, and the freedom to opt out, and disrupts the institutions that stand in the way of all three.

The two models of freedom give it away. One side wants freedom from the state: individual choice, market competition, personal autonomy deciding the outcome, the market as the curator. The other wants freedom through the state: public authority managing systems, protecting the vulnerable, regulating the market. Neither wants freedom from the building; both want control of different rooms inside it, and each accuses the other of setting fire to the parts it does not use.

The labels survive because the tribalism needs them, but the map the labels draw is wrong. A policy that protects incumbent wealth and existing aesthetics at the expense of newcomers is conservative no matter which party signs it, and a policy that opens the doors is progressive no matter who objects or which caucus claims credit for it.

The Mislabeled Door

This is why the parties keep talking past each other, and why the conversation never lands. Each side accuses the other of destroying what it loves while missing that both are curators, maintaining a collection and defending a wing, and that a curator who never locked a door is not a curator at all. The left will not surrender the natural world or the safety net; the right will not surrender autonomy or property; and the fight over which wings stay open will outlast every argument about which party believes in progress, because the fight is the job. Neither side wants the roof gone, and both know it, which is why the argument stays so loud.

The museum is not going anywhere. Only the curators rotate, and each generation hangs a new sign claiming the whole building was always theirs, and the old wing it closed is never mentioned again. When the French Revolution needed a building it could trust, it locked the crown’s collection in the king’s own palace and hung a new sign over the door; the paintings stayed, and only the labels changed.

Nobody is screaming about demolition; they are screaming about the roof and the plumbing and the admission prices, and the fight was never about demolition at all — it is about which wings stay open, which collections are preserved, and whose inventory the museum will hang when the lights come on tomorrow morning. It is about who gets to write the labels.
