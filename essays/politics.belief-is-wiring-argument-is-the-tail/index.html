Two Thermostats

Why political stances are visceral neurological tilts that the rational brain post-rationalizes, and why proven not to work is usually a metric mismatch

  • moral-foundations
  • neurology
  • amygdala
  • post-rationalization
  • metric-mismatch
  • politics

Winter in a rental, two thermostats on opposite walls, and the two people who set them are equally certain the other one is broken. Neither has touched the wiring; neither has seen the other’s dial.

Two thermostats in the same room, set to different temperatures, can both be right, and that is the cleanest way to read political disagreement. It is not two rational arguments about facts; it is two visceral tilts that have learned to argue. The tilt is the engine, and the argument is the brochure the engine prints to describe itself.

Watch two people debate for an hour and you will see them mistake post-rationalizations for reasoning. Nobody wakes up, consults a table of moral foundations, and then decides how to feel about tax policy; the stance arrives first, and the argument is assembled afterward to explain it.

Jonathan Haidt’s image is the precise one: the emotional dog wags the rational tail, never the reverse. The tail is talented; it can draft a brief for either side. It can draft the other side too.

The two tilts run on different wetware, if the imaging is to be believed: brain studies suggest that threat-sensitive temperaments react more sharply to ambiguity and that novelty-seeking runs the other pole. The science is real but still young; treat it as a lead, not a verdict.

The Wetware

Start with the amygdala, the alarm. Present a threat — a loud noise, an unfamiliar face, a break in the expected order — and some brains fire more intensely, and some barely register it, and neither brain chose its threshold. The high-alarm brain reads the world as a place where rules, borders, and clear norms are what keep people alive. Disrupting them feels like opening the door to the thing the alarm is for, and no amount of pointing at data will convince it otherwise.

The other pole runs through the anterior cingulate cortex, the part that notices when existing rules are not working and adjusts. The novelty-seeking brain lives there, restless when the system is rigid, quick to try the untested food or the untried trade. It reads a strict hierarchy as a machine for manufacturing avoidable suffering and the alarm brain’s caution as cowardice.

Both are survival strategies that kept tribes alive — the one that would not try the new berry and the one that would, balanced across generations. Descendants of both are still arguing in the same room.

Set the dials where you like; the argument will not move them.

The Explanation Engine

Watch what happens next. The visceral reaction fires in milliseconds. A moment later the prefrontal cortex wakes up and writes the story of why you feel that way — a brief about borders and rule of law, or a brief about human rights and shared prosperity.

Take immigration, the cleanest case. The alarm brain feels unchecked borders as a break in structure, a crack in the predictable order, and produces a policy position about national security. The novelty brain feels a wall as an arbitrary rule causing visible human suffering, and produces a position about universal rights. Neither person consulted the economics or the law; they consulted the amygdala and then hired the prefrontal cortex to argue the case.

The dog wags the tail, and the tail is very well educated. That is why two intelligent people can debate a policy for an hour and never once touch the thing actually deciding.

The argument is the tail, and it is wagging with total conviction.

The Metric-Mismatch Illusion

Which is where the complaint about policies that are proven not to work deserves a second look. ‘Proven not to work’ is always measured against some metric, and the metric belongs to the person measuring. When a group keeps pushing something that looks counterproductive on paper, it is rarely a logic failure; it is usually a collision between two definitions of what working means.

Rent control is the strongest move. By the market’s metric it fails: less supply, worse units, higher prices in the uncontrolled buildings, a slow bleed out of the whole neighborhood’s stock. By the tenant’s metric it works: the family in the gentrifying block stays in its home this month, and this month is the whole point. Both are measuring the same law with different instruments, and neither instrument is broken.

The same split runs through every policy fight. One side scores outcomes, the other scores principles, and a law that defends a principle keeps its defenders even when the outcome metric screams. Abandoning the principle to get a cleaner number reads to them as a compromise of integrity.

Then there is the incomplete-implementation fallacy, the universal solvent. When a policy fails, both sides blame the execution — not enough funding, a watered-down bill, spending not cut far enough — never the theory itself.

The brain protects its model by calling the real-world trial an incomplete test.

So the same brain that drafted the brief will also draft the excuse. It is defending an internal priority that no metric can touch — immediate harm, procedural rights, standing on principle. The policy was never trying to be what the critic thinks a policy should be. You are measuring against Objective A; they are solving for Objective B.

Two thermostats, same room, each set differently, each certain the other is malfunctioning — and neither one wrong. The room stays whatever it is, and neither dial moves.
