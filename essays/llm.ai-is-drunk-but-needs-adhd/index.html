AI Is Drunk, but It Needs ADHD

The industry's creativity slider just makes models drunk — real creativity is ADHD-style conceptual jumping, low randomness within a thought and clean macro-jumps between topics

  • llm
  • adhd
  • creativity
  • temperature
  • mixture-of-experts

At a demo, someone drags the temperature slider to the top of its track and calls the result creativity, and the model, asked for a sentence, delivers one that is technically English and philosophically a seizure. The screen shows a single dial hidden behind an interface most users never open, telling the model to stop betting on the most likely next word and reach further down the probability menu. Crank it high enough and the sky stops being blue and becomes an elephant.

That is the whole mechanism the industry calls creativity, and it is a randomness slider, a volume knob in a place where the design brief asked for a gearshift. Real creative thinking works the opposite way, and the closest human model for it sits in a psychiatric manual: ADHD, an engine whose track switches flip constantly, every train running perfectly at full speed while it jumps tracks.

The two states are not the same kind of failure.

Temperature Is a Word-Level Slider

An LLM is a guessing machine, the same prediction your phone makes when it suggests the next word, scaled up to something that looks like a brain. Type a sentence and it produces a menu of probabilities for the next word: after ‘the sky is,’ the model offers blue at 90 percent, bright at 7 percent, and elephant at a probability that rounds to nothing, a figure so small it might as well be a typo. Temperature is a dial, set somewhere between zero and one or two, that says how far down that menu the model may reach, and it is hidden behind the interface, so most people who use these models every day never see it.

At zero it always takes the top pick — hyper-logical, predictable, great for code and math, and dull within a paragraph. At high settings it grabs words near the bottom, which is how you get a sentence that is technically English and philosophically a seizure, the words in a row and the meaning in the ditch. This is word-level noise, the machine version of a drunk brain flooding with GABA and slowing its excitatory signals until logic breaks down word by word. It is not a creative leap; it is static.

The drunk metaphor is not a joke; it is the right physics, and the resemblance is anatomical. Alcohol floods the brain with GABA, an inhibitor, and slows glutamate, an exciter, and the signal stops getting through clearly; out come forgotten words, slurred syllables, sentences that collapse syllable by syllable. That is precisely what a high temperature does to a model, and it is why nobody mistakes a drunk for a genius, and it is why nobody should mistake a seizure for a style.

The Train That Jumps Tracks

The ADHD brain inverts that. Inside any single thought it is fully coherent, high-resolution, low-randomness, the train running smoothly at full speed, every car coupled, every line on time; what flips is the switch at the end of the line, because the gatekeeper that keeps a neurotypical brain locked on task is fast asleep, and the doors to unrelated thought stand open.

A line producer coordinating five hundred extras and crew across a vast desert set thinks in spreadsheets, walkie-talkies, and a central command tower — rigid, information funneling to one point, prone to bottlenecks — until mid-thought the filter drops and the brain flashes to a nature documentary about ants. Ants have no director and no megaphone; they self-organize in real time on pheromone trails, and the brain smashes the two worlds together: give every extra a digital map that drops virtual pheromones, and when a sector gets crowded the crowd routes itself, no walkie-talkie required. It is not a loss of focus; it is cross-pollination, a cross-industry search engine running a query nobody typed. Ask a current model to optimize that movie set and it stays inside the Hollywood textbook, because its attention filters are built to keep it on topic.

A model will not look at an ant colony unless you type the word — a machine terrified of changing the subject.

The chemistry of the nineteenth century had the answer in a dream: Kekulé watched a snake seize its own tail and woke knowing benzene was a ring, a jump from reptile to molecule that no prompt had suggested and no textbook had connected. The idea arrived as a class change, one domain offering the shape another was missing, the switch thrown between two freight yards. The router that prizes staying on topic would have filed that dream under noise.

The Router That Buries the Leap

Computer scientists read human mind-wandering as a bug to fix, and the field’s answer is a Mixture of Experts, the architecture behind models like DeepSeek: instead of one giant network, a panel of hyper-specialized linear thinkers — a math expert, a coding expert, a Hollywood expert — and a router that sends each prompt to the matching expert. On a movie-production question, the router hands the work to the Hollywood expert, and the biology expert, where the ant colonies live, stays in the dark; to be fair to the architecture, the experts do talk, their outputs combined and weighted through a gating network, and the problem is what the gate is trained to do.

It is trained to minimize loss, so it favors the predictable, statistically safe expert and drowns out the weird cross-domain handoff as inefficient noise; the jump from movie sets to ants is treated as a mistake to filter, not a breakthrough to keep. The connections a Mixture of Experts makes are always too close to the original industry, which is precisely where a creative leap never happens, because a leap by definition leaves the industry. So the tool of record for creative AI is a randomness slider, and the mechanism that actually produced the leaps is classified as a disorder.

Schools and workplaces spent decades telling the ADHD brain its filters were broken, a bug to be cured rather than a feature; those filters are the missing feature — low randomness within a thought, high connectivity between domains, clean macro-jumps between topics, structured chaos instead of random chaos. Build a mind-wandering layer, a background process whose entire job is to pull distant, unrelated concepts from entirely different data buckets and force the model to find the hidden structural similarity, and the machine stops being a flawless linear calculator that repeats the past and learns to daydream, the train finally allowed to take a different line.

The train is already moving; the industry only refuses to flip the switch.
