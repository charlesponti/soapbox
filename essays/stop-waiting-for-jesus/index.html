Stop Waiting for Jesus

The savior model of politics doesn't fail because we pick the wrong person. It fails because it's the wrong model.

  • politics
  • democracy
  • power
  • institutions
  • savior complex
  • exhaustion

Something happened to political discourse around the middle of the last decade that is difficult to name precisely but easy to recognize. Every election became existential. Every candidate became either a messiah or an antichrist. The stakes were always total, the outcome was always civilizational, and the emotional cost of participation was always exactly as high as it could possibly be. People who had never paid attention to politics could not look away. People who had always paid attention could not get out of bed.

The exhaustion is not incidental. It is the direct product of a particular way of understanding how political change works — a model so embedded in contemporary democratic culture that it rarely gets examined. The model goes like this: the right person, in the right office, can fix the thing that is broken. The task of citizens is to find that person, elect them, and then wait.

This is the savior model. It is also why nothing ever quite changes in the ways we’re told it will.

Trump crystallized this more vividly than any candidate in recent memory — not because of his particular ideology but because of the scale of the projections both sides placed on him. For his supporters, he was the figure who would finally say what no one else would say and do what no one else would do — a disruptor so total that the whole corrupted system would be forced to reckon with him. For his opponents, he was the figure who would finally end the democracy if not stopped — a threat so categorical that every election he appeared in was the last election that mattered. Both sides were running the same cognitive software. The candidate as vessel for total transformation. The election as apocalypse or salvation.

Neither projection was accurate. Not because Trump was unremarkable — he was not — but because no single figure in a constitutional system can deliver on what the savior model promises. The presidency operates inside a structure of laws, courts, legislatures, bureaucracies, local governments, and cultural institutions that were explicitly designed to resist exactly the kind of total transformation the savior model imagines. This is sometimes described as a failure of the system. It is the system working as intended.

The problem is not that we keep picking the wrong saviors. The problem is that we keep looking for one.

The savior model is seductive for reasons that are not stupid. Political systems are genuinely complex, genuinely difficult for individuals to influence, and genuinely structured in ways that concentrate power at the top. It is rational to focus attention on whoever holds that power. Leaders do matter — their decisions affect millions of people, their values shape the culture of their administrations, their choices about which fights to pick and which to avoid have real consequences.

But there is a difference between understanding that leadership matters and believing that the right leader solves the problem. The first is accurate. The second is a category error about how change actually moves through political systems.

Political change moves through institutions. It accumulates in laws, in precedents, in regulatory decisions, in the staffing of agencies that most people cannot name. It is built by people who show up to city council meetings and school board hearings and zoning reviews and primary elections for state legislature. It is sustained by organizations that exist between elections — that lobby, litigate, organize, and document across the years when no one is paying attention. The individual at the top of the system matters most when everything underneath that individual has been built to support a particular direction of change. Without the underneath, the individual is managing a different set of constraints than the one their supporters imagined they’d received.

The civil rights movement did not succeed because John F. Kennedy or Lyndon Johnson was the right person. It succeeded because decades of organizing, litigation, civil disobedience, and institution-building had created the conditions under which federal action became necessary and possible. The leaders at the top signed things. The change was built by people whose names are not on the legislation.

What the savior model costs is harder to see than what it promises, because the costs are absences.

The most concrete absence is political action between elections. If the candidate is the mechanism of change, then voting is the act, and everything afterward is waiting to see if it worked. The school board is someone else’s problem. The city zoning meeting is a detail. The state legislature race is too small to matter. The local organizing that would actually build the structural conditions for the policy the candidate promised — that is not the job. The job was done in November.

This is not laziness. It is the logical conclusion of a model that locates agency in the leader rather than in the citizenry. If change comes from the top, civic participation is a matter of selecting who stands at the top and then observing what they do. The waiting is built into the theory.

The second cost is structural blindness. When we focus on the personality at the top of a system, we stop examining the system the personality is operating inside. Housing is expensive not because the wrong person is in the White House but because of accumulated zoning decisions made by local governments over decades. Healthcare costs what it does not because of one administration’s policy but because of the incentive structures embedded in how hospitals, insurers, and pharmaceutical companies are regulated and reimbursed. These are institutional problems. They require institutional solutions. They will not be solved by electing someone who says the right things about them, because the person who says the right things will govern inside the same structure that produces the problems. The structure is the thing that needs to change. The savior model makes the structure invisible.

The third cost is the emotional one — the one that produces the exhaustion. If each election is existential, then losing is catastrophic and winning is never enough. The emotional stakes of the savior model are always total because the model promises total transformation. When the transformation doesn’t arrive — and it never fully arrives — the next election must offer even more total stakes to explain why the previous candidate failed. The cycle escalates. The exhaustion compounds. People who wanted to care about politics discover that caring in this way is unsustainable, and disengage entirely rather than find a different way of caring.

The alternative to the savior model is not detachment. It is not the knowing cynicism that treats all politics as performance and all politicians as interchangeable. That posture is just the savior model’s disappointed aftermath — the person who believed too much, got burned, and decided the answer was to believe in nothing.

The alternative is structural thinking. Not as an abstract preference but as a practical orientation: what institutions shape this outcome, what rules govern those institutions, who has power over those rules, and what would it take to change them? These questions are less emotionally satisfying than the question of whether the right person is about to be elected. They are also the questions that have answers.

Structural thinking leads to different places than savior thinking. It leads to local politics, which is where the most consequential decisions about land use, education, policing, and public health are actually made. It leads to the long game — to the understanding that changing a zoning code or a regulatory framework takes years of consistent pressure from people who show up repeatedly. It leads to institutions over personalities — to the organizations that exist between elections and do the work that elections cannot do.

The person at the top matters. And the person at the top operates inside a structure that was built by thousands of people who will never be on a ballot. If you want the structure to change, those are the people you need to become or to support. The election is the moment. Everything before and after it is the work.

No one is coming to save you from the work. That has always been true. The exhaustion is what happens when we spend enough time pretending otherwise.
