Culture Was Rationed

The old culture was a ration line, and clickbait, bad art, prestige, and awards are the ration board's words for what happens when people choose their own meal

  • culture
  • decentralization
  • attention
  • prestige

Culture used to be rationed. A studio, a label, a publisher stood at the front of the line and issued what the country would consume, stamped with the word prestige or the word essential, and you ate what you were given, in the portion you were given. Nobody called it a ration.

The ration was real and physical. A film print in a can, a vinyl record pressed in a plant, a book on paper — each cost real money to make, to ship, and to store, so a central committee had to decide what deserved to exist. Greenlight was the word for permission. The committee projected quotas, repeated safe formulas, and called its own taste the standard.

You did not choose from a market; you were issued a portion.

The End of the Line

The internet did not make a better ration. It ended the ration. Distribution fell to zero, which meant the greenlight committee was out of work, and the shelf became open to anyone with the nerve to stock it. YouTube is a free market in the literal sense: nothing between the maker and the buyer but the algorithm, which does not ask if a work deserves to exist, only if anyone wants it.

The algorithm is the ledger of supply and demand, written in real time. If a niche exists, no matter how odd, the market deploys a creator to fill it.

This is where the ration board’s vocabulary comes back as accusation. Clickbait is the first word. A thumbnail, a headline, a curiosity gap — the old gatekeepers call it manipulation, as if packaging were a new invention. It is a billboard, an album cover, a movie poster, a book jacket. Every one of those promised something it might not fully deliver, and none of them was ever called a crime.

The anger is not about the can; it is about having admitted you were hungry.

No Bad Food

Bad art is the second word, and it is the ration board’s most important. To call a work bad is to treat a subjective event as a measurement of matter. Tear a page from a notebook, pin it to a wall, and you have still staged a transaction with whoever stops to look; confusion, anger, and laughter are all outcomes of that transaction.

Art has no beauty molecules. It has no meaning atoms. Value is not inside the object; it happens in the space between the maker’s action and the observer’s attention, and it cannot be judged before it happens. When a critic rules a genre out, they are not describing art. They are describing the limits of their own tolerance, then dressing the limit up as a law.

The critic who survives this change is a proxy, not a priest. Their value is consistency: if their taste matches yours ninety percent of the time, they buy back your hours, and the review was never a verdict on the work — it was a signal to their own table.

A pan of a blockbuster is not a decree; it is the critic telling their own people the dish is not for them.

The Stamp Called Best

Prestige is the third word, and it is the cleverest stamp ever printed. It labels a subgenre — slow pacing, muted palettes, orchestral scores, existential themes, upper-middle-class worries — as if pacing and palettes were morality. It is a fashion house naming pajamas, feathers, and flippers the Elegant and billing it as taste itself. Nothing about that genre outranks a well-made slasher except the zip code of the people who prefer it.

The awards are the same stamp applied to people. Best Picture is a category error, a superlative pretending to be a measurement. An Oscar is closer to a certification — the mark on a manufactured part — signaling that a name is a safe bet, and the show needs the illusion of universality to keep selling tickets.

The panic over falling Oscar ratings is not a sign that culture is dying. It is the public noticing that the stamp was always local.

The Multipolar Meal

The solution is not to reform the Oscars or ban clickbait or standardize taste. It is to accept that the line is gone for good. Culture ends up with twenty awards for twenty aesthetic philosophies instead of one stamp for the whole country. A show that does not match your table is not an insult; it is a different table, and the rational response to a stranger’s table is not anger but apathy, because nobody at your table will starve.

The map changes too. The old line ran from a single capital out to the provinces, and prestige was measured by distance from the capital. The new map has no capital — a hundred towns, each the center of its own world, each issuing its own confidence index to its own investors.

What feels like the death of culture is the end of being told what to eat. What feels like chaos is the disappearance of the queue.

The ration books are in museums now, and the stamp is in your hand. No committee approves your appetite, and no board certifies your taste. You read the cans yourself, you bring your own table, and you are no longer obligated to consume what the line issued — which is not the loss of culture but the end of its rationing.
