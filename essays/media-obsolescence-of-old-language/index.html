The Obsolescence of Old Language

Television, streaming, movies, and media are copper in the wall — labels for a scarcity era that only two words still describe

  • language
  • platforms
  • content
  • streaming
  • technology

The last time you said you watched television, you were lying, technically. You sat in front of a laptop or a phone, picked from a catalog, pressed play. The word that came out of your mouth described the box, the schedule, the antenna — a machine that no longer carries your signal.

That is the shape of the problem: when a house switches from cable to the internet, the copper stays in the walls. It is still connected to the junction box on the street; nothing flows through it. Television is that copper. It goes nowhere, carries nothing, and keeps getting patched into new sentences.

The wiring is there; the signal left.

The Ghost Words

Television was born describing a physical thing: the cathode-ray tube, the antenna, the broadcast arriving on a schedule you did not choose, from a building you could not visit. That thing is gone. Netflix opened, and the word stayed behind like a label screwed to a dead outlet. It has meant nothing precise since.

Streaming was supposed to fix this. It described the technology — data flowing rather than arriving all at once — and the technology is invisible to the person watching.

The word streaming did one honest thing: it conceded that nothing is scheduled anymore. The content waits for you now, not the other way around. Then it described the least interesting part of the arrangement — how the data arrives — and said nothing about what actually changed, which is that you choose.

Media is the worst of the four. It means the platforms, and it means the works, and it means the industry, and it means a billboard. Nobody knows which. Once a media company meant a license and a channel; now every startup calls itself one, and the word survives by meaning everything and nothing.

Movie and show are artifacts of distribution: a two-hour reel for a theater, a scheduled episode for a schedule. A two-hour film on a phone is still called a movie, and a ten-minute episode is still called a show. The categories describe neither the format nor the experience anymore.

The same confusion runs in reverse between platforms. Netflix and a cable channel both offer a catalog you can open at will, and the differences between them matter to nobody who watches.

The language treats them as species.

Two Words

Strip the romance and the history and two things remain. A place where something lives, and the thing that lives there. That is the whole vocabulary: platform and content.

Platform makes no claim about the technology underneath. Netflix could deliver its shows by streaming, by download, by carrier pigeon, and it would still be a platform. Radio was a platform, libraries were platforms, billboards were platforms, books were platforms, and a conversation is a platform too. The word describes the function without constraining the machine.

Content is what you actually experience — the song, the article, the episode, the story. It does not care about duration, format, or the intention of the creator. It only has to appear somewhere, and the somewhere is a platform.

The simplicity is almost embarrassing.

The Scarcity That Made the Words

The old vocabulary was not built for this. It was built to describe scarcity. Broadcast television was scarce — a fixed number of channels, one thing in the air at a time, a night when the signal simply stopped. A movie was scarce — a theater, a reel, a print shipped in a can. A magazine was scarce — paper, ink, and a truck to deliver it.

The words named the limits, and the limits were real. Every channel limit, every gatekeeper, every scheduled hour was a physical fact, so the language that named them looked like the truth.

Those facts are gone. Anyone with a phone can reach the whole planet, and the whole planet can reach them back. There are no channel limits, no gatekeepers, no distinction between what a corporation produces and what an individual uploads. Both sit on the same platforms, reached the same way, and the old words keep insisting they are different species.

The words describe a world that no longer has a junction box.

The Delay

The transition will not be quick. Language is sticky, and institutions are stuck to the language, and the industry is held together by the old words. Newsrooms keep a TV department and a digital department as if the distinction meant something. Universities teach media studies, which is the study of platforms and content wearing an older coat. Job titles still say media when they mean production, distribution, or platforms.

The people who will retire the words are not in those institutions. They are the teenagers opening TikTok and saying they watched something. They never learned the categories because the categories never described their screen. They speak the simpler language already; the formal vocabulary is just late.

Twenty years from now the old words will read like wireless telegraphy. Technically accurate, historically true, and useful to nobody building anything.

The copper is still in the walls. You do not have to keep plugging into it. Say what you did: you opened a platform, you watched content, you listened to a story, you did not watch television. The signal left the wire years ago; the language can leave it too.
