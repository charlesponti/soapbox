Migration Is a Broken Feedback Loop

Why importing workers to fix a birth-rate collapse only deepens the housing trap that caused it

  • politics
  • migration
  • housing
  • demographics
  • feedback-loop

At two in the morning the boat sits low in the water, and the man on deck runs a pump whose intake hose trails over the rail into the sea, pouring water into the hold to hold the waterline where it is, because the seam below has been draining the boat all winter. He watches the level hold, then rise, and he pumps faster, and it does not occur to him that he is bailing by pouring.

Migration is the pump on a sinking boat.

The leak is demographic. Birth rates across the West sit below the two-point-one children per woman needed to hold a population steady, so the ratio of working taxpayers to pension and healthcare dependents shrinks with every retiring cohort. Rather than raise the birth rate, governments raise the water line: they import working-age adults, already born, already educated, paying taxes this quarter instead of twenty years from now, and counting the injection as a fix.

The aggregate is the alibi.

The Leak

The birth-rate collapse is not a cultural turn against family. Survey after survey finds that most young adults still want two or more children and feel financially unable to have them, and the gap between the wanting and the numbers is the whole story. The two reasons are government-made: higher education loads people with debt before adulthood begins, and housing prices have run so far past wages that settling down feels like a bet against homelessness. Young people read those odds every month, and family formation needs two things — physical space and the confidence that a rent hike will not evict the plan.

Housing is the single largest predictor of whether a family forms. Delay leaving home, delay the partnership, delay the first child, and by the time the numbers work the window for a second and a third has closed. The whole arrangement carries a latent risk of homelessness, and the risk is the point; the replacement rate was not lost to ideology, it was lost to the balance sheet.

The fiscal ledger tells the same story: a high-skilled arrival is a net contributor from day one, while a lower-wage arrival, foreign or domestic, draws more in services, tax credits, and infrastructure than it pays in direct income tax. Net contribution sits at neutral or negative, and the worker who is supposed to rescue the pension age is, on average, another line on the service bill.

The policy pitch assumes a precision administration cannot deliver. A government that says it needs fifty thousand care workers does not get fifty thousand care workers; it opens a category and receives hundreds of thousands, most of whom follow wages and geography toward other work. Headline arrivals include spouses and children, so a net figure of five hundred thousand is not five hundred thousand workers, and every arrival is an immediate bid on a house, a school place, a train seat — the targeting was the argument, and volume destroys the targeting.

The Loop

Now add the pump. The imported worker is exactly the person who needs the scarce thing, a home, and in supply-constrained cities where zoning throttles building, every new arrival bids against every native who wants one. Housing gets more expensive, which makes family formation harder, which lowers the birth rate further, which requires more import — immigrants land in the same financial headwinds, and their fertility falls to match the native low, and the loop is closed: the water being pumped in is the water making the boat sink.

The numbers that justify the pump are aggregate, and aggregates lie about individuals: GDP rises with every head that lands even as the median person treads water, and the same accounting that celebrates total output hides per-capita stagnation and the deterioration of the things output is supposed to buy. Aggregate thinking also underrates the asymmetric — nineteen hijackers once changed the course of history, and no rate-based model flagged them as signal, because a population policy that counts only the total cannot feel the leak, and a leak you cannot feel is the one that sinks you.

The Pump House

The pump is not held by idiots; it is held by people whose horizon is the next election, and migration fixes a labor shortage this afternoon while housing supply takes fifteen years to build and a newborn takes twenty to become a taxpayer. The Treasury wants the tax base now; the housing department inherits the ten-year consequence; nobody is rewarded for the meeting where a trade-off is admitted out loud. Cheap, ready labor also mutes the incentive to automate, so the low-wage model that migration patches is the same model migration preserves. The pump runs because it is the only policy that works within a political lifetime.

The honest version of the debate is a choice between two paths. Population expansion accepts expensive housing and low native fertility while trying to build enough high-density homes to keep up; structural reform relaxes land rules, relieves education debt, and supports young households, at the cost of a tighter labor market in the short run. One path feeds the pump, the other patches the leak, and the second one is the repair — and repair is slow.

Every year the pump runs, the leak gets wider, and the case for more pumping gets stronger. The engineers will keep arguing about pump capacity, right up until the waterline argues back.
