The Moralizing Controller

Why a televangelist banning video games and a regulator mandating theatrical windows run identical code

  • economics
  • media
  • regulation
  • censorship
  • consumer-sovereignty

Nine at night, and the couch has won again. The film is streaming, the two tickets on the dresser went unused, and a family that was supposed to be in the dark of a theater sits instead in the light of its own living room. Somewhere downtown a deputy attorney general is drafting the rule that would push them back outside, past the parking lot, into seats at twenty dollars before the popcorn. The family was not consulted. It does not know it is the reason a lawsuit exists.

The dam is the same dam. Build a wall across a river of consumer choice and call it public morality, or cultural integrity, or protecting the vulnerable from themselves — the wall only buys time. The water never stops looking for the low ground, and the low ground is a streaming service, a ninety-day window, a ten-dollar rental, a hundred thousand independent feeds, a world of content you never had to approve. The water will find it.

Mortal Kombat in 1992, theatrical windows in 2026. Different century, identical mechanics. The pastor said the game corrupted the youth and demanded the ban to protect them; the technocrat says the couch corrupts cinema and mandates the window to protect the culture. Both hold a preference, and both mistake it for a law of nature. The attorneys general pressing the theatrical-window case against Paramount and Warner Bros are not defending the audience or the art; they are defending a distribution model that lost the argument to the couch.

Journalists and artists have forgotten their job, which is to give people what they want, not what the creator wants or what the institution has decided they should want. They have elevated themselves to a god-like position where consumers are supposed to absorb whatever they are given and be grateful for it. The drive-in got no regulations, no emergency declarations, no attorneys general, because it never threatened anyone’s claim to superiority.

Moralizing is the confession of lost leverage.

The Same Dam

When an incumbent cannot win on price or utility, it reframes preference as moral failure, and the water behind the wall presses a little higher. The public is not choosing something else; the public is uncultivated. The family that stays home to stream is not responding to cost and convenience; it is failing, and the correct response to that failure is not a better product but a lecture, a subsidy, or a mandate.

The doctrine is interchangeable. A man in a lifted truck flying a flag, a trans goth socialist, a Hollywood writer marching in high-end activewear — the beliefs contradict, the function does not. Each adopted whichever framework promised the best return on ego for the least effort. When the market gets to decide what is valuable, self-appointed arbiters lose their claim to being special, so they refuse to let the market decide. Status is the real currency, and taste is how they buy it, and no market argument survives contact with a wounded ego.

The content of the orthodoxy never matters; the method does.

The Water Remembers the Low Ground

History is a graveyard of displaced formats, and the water remembers every channel it carved before the wall went up. Radio was supposed to destroy live music; it moved music into the speakers where people already were. Television was supposed to kill the motion picture; the picture kept moving. Studios sued VHS over claims that it would wreck the film ecosystem, and VHS built the home-video business that out-earned theaters for years, with no subsidy and no savior. When the drive-in died, when vaudeville collapsed, when photography upended portrait painting, no attorney general declared a cultural emergency.

Today the formats that are failing are suddenly sacred trusts that must be saved from the very people who consume them. Every time a technology lowers friction, the incumbent calls the new medium a threat to art. Every time, the consumer wins, because frictionless access to what people actually want beats forced compliance.

The market is a voting system, and consumers make the rules because consumers pay the bills. A family that would rather watch a two-hour drama on a couch than spend twenty dollars on a ticket, parking, and overpriced popcorn and call it culture is not making an error; it is expressing a rational preference for comfort, cost, and control. You cannot regulate human desire. That is why every attempt to legislate the audience back into seats, and every subsidy for a format the public has left, ends in the same place.

Reality does not care about your moral framework, and art and journalism do not exist in a vacuum above the market. Creators who elevate themselves to god-like arbiters of taste are left behind by the very audiences they claim to serve, and the audiences do not notice the departure.

The Mirror They Refuse

Point out the parallel and the regulator rejects it instantly, arguing content instead of method, while a hairline crack runs down the wall where the water has leaned for years. The pastor had the Word of God; the technocrat has the academic consensus. Both are convinced that a different doctrine changes the behavior, and neither notices that the behavior, the coercion, and the refusal to listen are identical.

Force an institution and you do not program the reader; you arm the skeptic.

Even the fantasy fails. Suppose the wall held and every American was forced back to the theater and forced to read a daily paper. People who know they are being steered seek out the antithesis as an act of defiance, so forced readership breeds contrarian readers, not believers, and mandated cinema breeds audiences who hate the theater. You can control the container. You cannot control the mind inside it, and every mandate makes the mind more hostile to the mandate.

The water does not change because you built the dam. It finds the low ground, and on the far side of the wall it becomes a different river. The dam was never the point; the river was.
