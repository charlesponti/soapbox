Equality Is a Conditional Weapon

Equality is never absolute — it bends to whoever sits inside the moral circle, which makes it recruitment rhetoric, not a principle

  • politics
  • power
  • morality
  • equality
  • dehumanization

There is a clock in the stairwell of an old courthouse whose pendulum never stops at the center of its swing. It passes the vertical mark, keeps moving, holds a beat on the far side, and starts back from where it landed, and if you stand there long enough the weight spends more time on one side than the other while the face still says it is keeping time. Downstairs, in the hearing room, a lawyer is using the word that holds the building together, and every person at the railing hears it pointed at someone specific.

Equality is the pendulum of that clock, and no one believes in it.

If half the population were violent murderers and the other half were not, no one would ask for perfect balance between them; the question would be how fast the other half could organize. The word bends to whoever is inside the circle, and the circle is drawn before any principle is consulted. Every swing passes the vertical mark, past balance, and settles somewhere on the far side; the clock never rests at the center, and each overshoot becomes the next grievance, the next swing beginning from where the last one landed. The mechanism is older than any flag, any ideology, any constitution — it only looks like balance because the swing takes years and the audience has a short memory.

Consider what the word actually does in a room. It is invoked most loudly by the group that currently holds the smaller share of whatever is being divided, and it is always aimed at the group holding the larger one. The speaker rarely wants half; the speaker wants the share to move — equality is the request, and redistribution is the demand.

The Moral Circle

The circle is drawn by whoever holds the weaker position, and it is drawn in self-defense, because no underdog has ever won a war of hard power. A group without hard power cannot attack the rulers power-for-power; it cannot touch the corporations, the military, or the courts. So it does the only thing left: it attacks the ruler’s identity, making the dominant group’s very existence synonymous with evil, its every trait recast as a vice. Once a person is dehumanized, stripping them of everything becomes justified, and no one doing the stripping has to feel bad about it — the standard playbook of the underdog, updated with modern vocabulary.

The machinery is older than any movement. It is the same evolutionary tool that let one band of humans slaughter another, now aimed at a colleague’s reputation instead of a rival’s throat.

Dehumanization is the only weapon the weak can swing.

The loop is older than any current movement, and it runs in four steps. Group A wins the historical lottery, builds a system that rewards itself, and writes a moral story that makes its dominance look natural and inevitable. Group B, feeling precarious under that system, weaponizes morality, turning A’s traits into vices and its own into virtues, and uses that leverage to capture power — but because it had to dehumanize A to win, it does not stop at fifty-fifty. It keeps going, becomes the new heavy-handed ruler, and the remnants of A begin the same process from the other side.

The clean fifty-fifty has never existed in any society worth the word. Every arrangement that calls itself equal has a class that does not count, a population quietly outside the circle — the ones the pendulum does not carry — and the circle moves as the weights shift, which is why the grievance list is never empty. Someone is always the new evil, and someone is always the new victim.

Each overshoot resets the clock.

Recruitment, Not Principle

That is why the word equality does so much work and so little. It is rarely the actual goal of a mass movement; it is the romantic slogan used to recruit the quiet eighty percent and demoralize the ruling class, and it works because it sounds like a principle. In the cold math of history, movements are not driving toward a grand, peaceful equilibrium; they are driving toward resource and power capture, and changing the dictionary is the cheapest way to clear the path. The twenty percent who actually run the movement understand this perfectly well; they only need the eighty percent to believe the word is the destination.

The proof comes after capture. The movement that wins does not install the equality it promised; it installs itself. The old rulers’ vices become the new rulers’ virtues, and the anthem stays in the anthem.

Watch what the winning side does with the word after it wins. Equality becomes a test for admission, not a promise of treatment.

If equality were the goal, the pendulum would stop at vertical.

The Cost of the Circle

It reads as cynicism because every movement tells itself the same story: its own overshoot is different, this time the circle includes everyone, and this time the pendulum will hold. The rulers always believed the same thing about themselves, and that conviction is exactly what makes them rulers, and also why the swing never stops. Every incumbent in history has believed it was the exception to the pendulum, and every incumbent has been the next grievance instead. Robespierre ran the Terror as a machine of virtue and watched the machine ask for his own head — the mechanism is indifferent to the moral quality of the side it serves.

The swing is not a failure of intention; it is a feature of the mechanism. A clock that rested at vertical would be a different machine entirely.

None of this is an argument for staying still. It is an argument for noticing what the word is actually doing when it is spoken. The circle is not a bug in the system; it is the system, and the only honest question about any movement is not whether it wants equality, but who it has decided does not deserve it, and what happens to them when the swing lands.

No one has ever built a clock that rests at vertical, and no movement has ever wanted to.
