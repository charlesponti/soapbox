Silence Killed Productivity, Not Remote Work

  • work
  • productivity
  • engineering
  • management

When a development team’s output drops after going remote, the standard diagnosis is environmental: the disruptions of working from home, the loss of office infrastructure, the stress of the transition. This leads to environmental interventions — better tooling, stricter standups, more structured async communication. Sometimes these help marginally. Often the decline continues.

The environmental diagnosis is usually wrong.

In the team I was working with, productivity declined for months after the remote transition despite exactly these kinds of interventions. We added structure. We improved the tooling. We set clearer expectations. The decline continued. When I finally looked at what had actually changed, the answer wasn’t in any of the things we’d changed — it was in something we hadn’t noticed we’d lost.

The Signal We Never Named

In an office, developers absorb a continuous low-level feed of information about whether their work matters. They overhear a sales call where a prospect asks about a feature they just shipped. They see an account manager’s face when they explain a bug fix. They catch a hallway conversation about what users are confused by. None of this is deliberate. None of it is in a meeting or a document. It’s ambient — a background signal so constant that no one notices it’s providing information.

Remote work eliminates it entirely.

The team hadn’t lost motivation in the abstract. They’d lost access to the answer to a specific question: does this work matter to anyone? In an office, that question gets answered incidentally, dozens of times a day, by noise you didn’t even know you were processing. Working from home, it goes silent. You ship features into a void. You fix bugs without seeing their impact. You make decisions without any signal about whether they were right.

The productivity drop wasn’t about focus or environment. It was about information poverty. Engineers who can’t perceive the effect of their work will eventually stop caring about the quality of it — not because they’re lazy or disengaged, but because quality requires feedback and there was none.

The Wrong Prescription

The standard remote-work productivity playbook treats the problem as attention management: reduce distractions, structure time, add accountability. This addresses a real problem (remote work does create distraction challenges) but entirely misses the purpose-signal problem. You can eliminate every distraction and give engineers perfect focus, and if they’re still building in a void, the output quality will still degrade.

Adding more structure to a team suffering from purpose disconnection makes it worse. More standups, more tracking, more documentation — these look like management but they’re noise. They add overhead without adding signal. The engineers now have less time and the same amount of purposelessness.

Restoring the Signal

The intervention that actually worked was simple and non-obvious: put engineers in direct contact with users.

Not through polished research reports. Not through product specifications translated from user feedback. Directly — in the room (or on the call) when users described what was working, what was confusing, and what they needed. Engineers heard users mention features they’d built. They heard frustration about things they could fix. They heard enthusiasm that was specific and credible because it was unmediated.

The motivational effect was immediate and not subtle. Engineers who had been building in a void were now building toward something they could see. The feedback loop that office environments had provided incidentally was now being provided deliberately.

The insight is transferable: when productivity declines in a remote team, before diagnosing attention or process, ask what information the team is missing. If they can’t answer “does this matter?” with confidence, the intervention is informational, not structural. Restore the signal. Everything else is noise.
