Dynamic Pricing Is an Auction

Why dynamic pricing is a rationing auction where you bid against other passengers, not against the airline

  • dynamic-pricing
  • travel
  • economics
  • airlines
  • consumer-surplus
  • scalping

Eleven at night, and you are looking at the same flight you priced this morning, and the number on the right side of the screen is $212 higher than it was at breakfast. Same plane, same window seat, same forty thousand feet — only the number has moved, and the number moved because someone else, a consultant or a family emergency or a wedding you will never be invited to, has been looking at it too. Your cursor hovers over the button. You are not deciding whether to buy a seat. You are deciding whether to raise your hand.

The airline is not your adversary; it is the auctioneer.

The room is the cabin, and the room is an auction. One hundred fifty seats, one hundred fifty paddles — one for every passenger who wants to be in New York at that particular hour. The airline stands at the front of the room holding the gavel. It does not bid; it only takes the bids and repeats them so the room can hear, and the price climbs because the room keeps deciding that this hour matters more than the last one. When you complain about the fare you imagine a duel with a machine, one-on-one and fair — but look across the cabin: a hundred and forty-nine people want the seat too, and the machine in the middle is only recording who wants it most.

Price is not a moral statement about what a seat should cost. It is a rationing mechanism for something finite.

A Boeing 737 carries somewhere between 160 and 200 seats, and the seat you are not sitting in is gone forever the moment the plane pushes back. Demand spikes; capacity does not. A Sunday-evening flight before the work week draws a different crowd than a Tuesday afternoon flight, and the same seat is worth different things to each of them — to the consultant who has to be in the office, to the woman flying to a funeral, to the couple who booked in February because it was cheap. Somebody gets the seat. The only honest question is who.

Static pricing does not remove the auction; it moves the auction to a worse venue.

Static Pricing Is Time-Rationing

Suppose the airline did what you actually want it to do and set a flat, low price on every seat to relieve your anxiety. Run the scene forward. The flight sells out the minute it goes on sale, the way a concert tour sells out, and the seats go to whoever happened to be sitting at a computer when the sale opened — six months early, on a Tuesday morning, before the work week had even started. The consultant who needs to fly tomorrow, the man whose mother is dying in Cleveland, the bride whose flight leaves in forty-eight hours: all of them are locked out, no matter what they would have paid. You have not made travel fair. You have replaced a price-rationed market with a time-rationed one.

The price-rationed market allocates by valuation. The time-rationed market allocates by whoever has the fastest fingers on a Tuesday morning. The auctioneer never left; he only moved the sale to a worse hour and put the gavel away.

The Scalper Never Left

The concert industry ran the static experiment for decades, because artists were afraid of alienating fans. Watch how it worked. A face-value ticket at $150; a super-fan willing to pay $1,200. Brokers bought the cheap tickets and flipped them on StubHub, pocketing the $1,050 difference, while the artist and the venue collected exactly zero of it — a second auction held in the parking lot, run by a different auctioneer, who kept the whole increment. Underpricing a hard-capacity asset does not create cheap access. It creates a middleman who charges the market price anyway and keeps the margin.

Airlines simply internalized the scalper. The late-booking business traveler who pays $1,200 is paying the same $1,050 premium, except it goes to the airline’s revenue instead of a broker’s markup. Dynamic pricing is scalping with the middleman cut out, and the consumer surplus — the difference between what a passenger will pay and what they do pay — stays where it belongs, in the room where the gavel actually falls.

This is why the Southwest experiment ended. For decades Southwest ran flat, distance-based pricing and became the most consistently profitable airline in history, growing a demographic that had never flown. It worked until Wall Street noticed the underexploited revenue. Do the arithmetic the way a revenue manager does: sell twenty of a hundred empty seats at $100 and take in $2,000, but lose the $400 that a $500 traveler would have paid, and watch the whole cabin fill with bargain hunters who have learned to wait.

Airlines would rather fly twenty seats empty than risk a high-value passenger catching a low price. Revenue cannibalization, not spite, is what killed flat pricing.

The same fear is why static pricing cannot work for concerts either. Venues resisted for years, and the resistance just handed the profit to brokers; Ticketmaster now sells “Platinum” seats at market price out of self-defense. An industry that tries to eliminate dynamic pricing on a scarce asset does not get stability. It gets a third party that re-introduces the dynamic pricing and keeps the spread.

The Only Coherent Position

Here is the tell that the “scam” story is wrong: nobody actually wants static pricing for everyone. They want static pricing for themselves.

Set a low, fixed price for one traveler on a peak holiday flight and leave the market dynamic for everyone else, and that traveler wins everything — low cost, zero anxiety — while the airline charges everyone else more to cover the loss. If everyone gets the low, fixed price, the dynamic market does not turn into universal cheap travel. It turns into instant sellouts, waiting lists, and a black market where scalpers re-introduce the dynamic pricing you tried to abolish. Fixed for me, dynamic for thee is the only version of the static argument that benefits anyone, which is why the argument always comes from a single paddle in the back row, never from the whole room.

The auction is not a flaw in the system. It is the system, and it has always been the system — every bazaar, every trade route, every harvest negotiated in real time against the person beside you, priced by need, by supply, by the hour of the day. In the open-outcry pits of Chicago, the men in colored jackets shouted at one another and not at the exchange; the exchange never set the price, it only recorded the moment the room agreed on one. A printed price tag is the historical anomaly, and it is only an anomaly because the goods behind it can be manufactured again tomorrow. A seat on a plane cannot.

You are not being cheated. You are being outbid.

Then the gavel falls.
