The Wedding Tax

Why the feature-film label adds five to ten times the cost of identical minutes that television builds for a fraction of the price

  • economics
  • film
  • hollywood
  • prestige-tax
  • media

Six in the morning on a soundstage, and three hundred to five hundred people are waiting for a light to stop flickering. The gaffer works from a lift, the script supervisor marks the take, and a producer’s assistant carries a budget that runs to nine figures. The frame holds 172,800 still images and two channels of audio either way. Somewhere a small crew is making the same minutes for a fraction of the money, and no screen will ever reveal which room they came from.

A caterer prices a hundred-person birthday party at one figure, and the same room, the same chicken, the same cake, at four times that once the word wedding is spoken. The chicken has not changed. Only the context has — and with it, the tolerance for waste.

Hollywood has collected that markup for a century and called the difference cinema. Classify a script as a feature film and every vendor, every agent, every union line item slaps on a prestige tax. Slice the identical 120 minutes into four episodes and the work gets built for a fraction of the price: a season of The Bear, Beef, or a prestige foreign hit shoots for $3 million to $5 million an episode with the same cameras, the same lighting, the same craft. The same medium, the same moving pictures, subjected to two entirely different ledgers.

A feature of the same length carries a number five to ten times higher, and no one in the room can say where the delta lives. Two hours of filmed story costs $150 million as a feature film and less than $10 million as two or three episodes of television. It does not live in the footage. Most of the $100 million is spent before the first frame is shot — soundstages at $20,000 a day, three hundred to five hundred people on set for months, producer layered on producer. Camera technology has been getting cheaper for twenty years and movie budgets have climbed three hundred percent, and nobody passes the savings along. The industry prices status, and status has no cost sheet.

The one real buyer of that status is the executive who signs the check.

The $15-Million Alibi

An actor who takes $250,000 to $500,000 an episode for four to six months of television — eight to ten hours of finished screen time — demands $15 million to $20 million for eight to ten weeks on a feature, a quarter of the running time and half the work days, three to five times the pay for a fraction of the work. The labor did not get harder. The category got taller.

Studios pay it as career insurance. Cast an unknown stage actor for $100,000 and a flop gets the executive fired, because the gamble is visible. Hire the A-lister for $15 million and a flop reads as bad luck, because the executive hired the biggest star in town, and the country club agrees it made sense on paper. Fifteen million buys board cover, not a better movie.

The market stopped honoring the policy. Barbie opened because it was Barbie, Mario because it was Mario, Oppenheimer because of Nolan and an atom bomb — not because of the star on the poster. Audiences buy concepts now; the accounting still prices the 1990s.

The Director Premium

The same misattribution canonized Greta Gerwig. Barbie grossed $1.4 billion on sixty years of brand equity, a $150-million marketing blitz, and two luminous leads, and being the first solo female director past the billion-dollar mark made the attribution feel inevitable. The honest split runs something like 60 percent brand, 25 percent marketing, 10 percent cast, the director’s execution at five to ten. A rational firm would note the split and keep the next budget disciplined. Hollywood priced the director as the whole package instead, and the board signed it.

Netflix handed her a reported $200 million to $300 million for Narnia, betting hundreds of millions on a single name. One outcome, mistaken for a skill.

In the auction house the same canvas is priced by the name stenciled on the back of the frame, and the paint underneath never changed.

The Ticket That Doesn’t Count

Critical acclaim cannot rescue the arithmetic. The Woman King scored 94 percent on Rotten Tomatoes and lost money in theaters — $50 million to make, tens of millions more to market, $125 million needed to break even after exhibitors took their cut, $92 million grossed worldwide. Five to six million people bought tickets at $11 to $13, less than two percent of the country — a real audience, and a real loss, invisible to any random poll and any streaming home screen.

The check got written anyway, because the tickets were never the whole point. A theatrical run certifies a title as cinema before it earns as a subscription anchor, and streaming services desperately need high-intent, targeted content to keep specific subscriber groups from canceling their plans. Every so often a targeted bet escapes its niche — Get Out at $4.5 million to $255 million, Crazy Rich Asians at $30 million to $238 million — and whispers that the lottery pays. A lottery that occasionally pays is still a lottery.

Nobody is forced to confront any of this, because the premium is never paid from the payer’s own pocket. Shareholder money, tech-balance-sheet money, subsidized money — the executive’s bonus clears either way, and the whole town has agreed the bet was reasonable. Waste is not a bug in this pricing model; waste is the product.

The same chicken feeds the same hundred people either way. Only the invoice knows the word.
