The AI Flywheel Needs the Workers

The AI ad-fueled flywheel only turns when the gains reach the workers who would spend them

  • economics
  • ai
  • productivity
  • labor
  • equity
  • advertising

Thursday afternoon, and the report that used to take ten days came back in one. The manager reads the summary, looks up, and says good, give us ten of these by Thursday. Nobody is told to take Friday off; the team is told the machine is working.

Think of the whole arrangement as a waterwheel. The wheel turns only when water is actually channeled onto its paddles; the flow has to arrive at the wheel, not disappear into a pipe upstream, and not pool in a reservoir that nobody drinks from. The theory of the AI economy is a closed loop: humans use AI, productivity rises, humans work less while holding more equity in the gains, the freed hours become attention that ads can be sold against, and the money from those ads becomes the demand that funds the next round of entrepreneurship. The loop is a real mechanism, and every link in it is checkable; the link that never gets inspected is the equity link — the one that moves the water from the top of the system to the wheel — and pull that link and the whole wheel stands still.

The gains reaching the workers is the entire flywheel.

The Historical Failure

The loop has a precedent, and the precedent failed. Keynes predicted that productivity would buy us 15-hour workweeks; instead a century of gains bought us busier schedules. The gains were real; they accrued to capital — to equity holders and to the platforms that sit between people and the economy — not to labor, and a gain that accrues to capital does not become demand, because it does not have to; it becomes an asset, priced, held, and watched rise while the people who made it keep working. For forty years the water has been diverted upstream, and the wheel has turned anyway because the base of the economy was wide enough to hide the diversion, until the base stopped being wide enough.

The links on either side of the equity link are sound. If people work less while holding real equity in the gains, the marginal cost of starting a business drops near zero and demand triggers a wave of micro-entrepreneurs building for niche audiences; media platforms thrive on idle hours, so a shift of work hours into leisure inflates the inventory of human attention, pushes ad prices down, and raises exposure. Money that circulates faster expands activity rather than pooling it.

The machine is real; the plumbing is the problem.

The Equity Hinge

The hinge is honest, so state it plainly: for the loop to run, one of two things must happen. Either policy intervenes — universal basic income, mandatory profit-sharing, a statutory shorter workweek — or ownership is broadened until non-labor income actually offsets the lost wage. Without one of those, the worker gets less time or lower real income, and the purchasing power that step six of the loop depends on never materializes, no matter how fast the machine can write a report. Every other link in the chain is downstream of this one.

Now watch the same dynamic from the inside of a job, where efficiency has a paradox attached to it: when production of a thing becomes cheaper, consumption of that thing rises. Jevons observed it for coal; AI performs it on work. A report that used to take ten days now takes one, and the company does not say take Friday off — it says give us ten reports by Thursday, and then a hundred. The freed time does not become leisure; it becomes more of the same labor, because the organization is not in the business of freeing time; it is in the business of throughput, and throughput answers to the owners of the wheel, not to the people who turn it. The medieval lord ran the same machine: the only mill in the village, the law that sent every sack of grain through it, and the miller’s share taken in flour.

The worker without equity is the water, never the miller.

What Freezes First

The obstacle is not ignorance of the mechanism; the mechanism is old and the diagnosis is public. It is incentive: the people who control the wheel are the people who benefit from the diversion, and the diversion is the status quo that forty years of gains built. Call it a cultural problem and the loop becomes a plea; call it an ownership problem and it becomes a conflict — most of the conversation stops at the plea, because the conflict has a price, and the people setting the price are the ones the loop would have to dispossess. The loop will be sold as inevitable in the meantime, a flywheel that turns on its own.

The failure mode is not a crash; it is a freeze — the wheel stops turning while everyone agrees it is spinning. Consumption plateaus, ad inventory rises against it, the marginal entrepreneur finds no demand at the end of the funnel, and the whole apparatus hums at full capacity producing gains for no one in particular, like a wheel spinning in an empty mill. The loop does not die loudly; it just stops feeding itself.

A gain that never reaches a spender is not growth; it is a balance-sheet entry.

The flywheel turns on demand, and demand is made of paychecks and dividends, not of valuations, and not of the press releases that measure the boom. Channel the gains down to the people who would spend them, and the water reaches the wheel; keep diverting it upstream, and every impressive measure of the AI boom will be a description of the wheel doing nothing.
