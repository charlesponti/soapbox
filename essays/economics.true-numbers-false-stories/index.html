True Numbers, False Stories

Why a writer can report a true number that conveys a false picture

  • economics
  • media
  • journalism
  • incentives
  • truth

Eleven at night, and a newsroom that should be dark is lit at one desk. The reporter on it is turning a press release into a headline, and the release says the tournament took in somewhere near $80 million. The number is meant to be heard as profit — a vault of cash in a back room while the athletes take the scraps. The figure is exact. The picture is a lie.

A clerk copies a ledger for a living, and he writes only the top line. The figure he copies is exact, and the number that matters, the bottom line, is never copied, so the ledger reads healthy when the business is bleeding. The clerk is not forging anything. He is just not writing the part that would change the story.

Every figure in the paragraph was true.

The Top Line

Eighty million sounds like a vault of cash sitting in a back room. Run a grand slam and the money goes to stadiums, security, hundreds of staff, insurance, technology, and utilities before a single dividend is paid, and none of that overhead ever makes a headline. If the tournament took in eighty million and spent seventy-five on operations, the hoarding story collapses into a modest margin. That is exactly why the revenue number is printed and the margin is not.

The outrage evaporates, and with it the clicks.

The arithmetic is easy enough to show. A company that earns a hundred million in revenue and spends two hundred to earn it loses a hundred million; the headline number is a loss in disguise, and the disguise is doing exactly the work it was designed to do. Any reader who has ever paid a bill can follow that. The writer is not paid to follow it. The incentives run to engagement, speed, and narrative alignment, and every one of them points at the top line. Nothing in the system pays for the bottom line, so the bottom line does not get written.

Add the big-number illusion. To a reader earning a normal salary, any number with million attached feels infinite, and a corporate revenue stream reads like a lottery win rather than a cost center that has to clear every year. A company taking in a hundred million can sit a month from bankruptcy. The headline never tells you which one it is.

The body count was America’s great experiment in this. The number ran every night during the war in Vietnam, and every figure was real — a battalion clerk counting actual bodies, a tarp over a truck in the heat — and the sum of all that accuracy told the country a story about progress that was false. No single number was wrong; the picture was.

The easy number is also the only number on offer. Revenue is blasted out in press releases, while true net margins hide inside complex structures, tax exemptions, and private ownership, and a journalist pumping out several pieces a day will not spend a week digging for a figure nobody asked for.

The Good Guy Filter

Calling this malice is the mistake. The journalist at the desk does not wake up planning to deceive anyone; they believe they are a good person doing good work, and mostly they are right. They are a clerk who genuinely believes the top line is the story.

The distortion happens because the writer thinks they are doing a good job.

The filter has three layers. Righteousness comes first: a big number in the service of the underdog validates a well-intentioned mission, and digging into operational costs feels like making excuses for the powerful, so the honest-sounding move is to stop digging. The newsroom shares the worldview, so a piece that concludes the margins are thin and the pay is fair reads as betrayal, and writers are wired to avoid that friction. Story comes last: a clean and punchy article is what a good writer produces, and tax structures, depreciation, and insurance premiums destroy the flow. The financial math is cut, not as a lie, but as boring administrative fluff, in service of the people.

Below the filter sits the education gap. Many writers chose language because their strengths ran to words, not data, and they were never trained to spot a flawed study, a misleading sample size, or the difference between correlation and causation. Because parsing raw data is hard and time is short, they lean on pre-chewed press releases from think tanks and corporations and copy the headline statistic without touching the math behind it. They are not hiding the bottom line; they genuinely cannot read it.

The Output of the System

Publishing the raw number is not a mistake; it is the logical output of what the system pays writers to do.

The economics on top of all this point the same way. Outlets are funded by clicks, views, and watch time, and a balanced breakdown of a statistic is rarely exciting; sensationalism, fear, and outrage drive the highest engagement. Even subscription models cater to the biases of the paying audience, because a writer who challenges the readers’ deepest beliefs risks the financial baseline. Nuance is the thing a career cannot afford. The whole apparatus is a machine built to print the top line and file the bottom line away.

The clerk copied the top line because copying the top line is the job.

This stays invisible because it looks like honesty. The number is real, the writer is sincere, and the story is compelling, and nothing in the final product flags itself as incomplete. The reader finishes the piece feeling informed and slightly righteous, holding a picture the ledger does not support, and the writer collects the credit for having told it.

A ledger copied only at the top line is a ledger copied wrong. Until someone pays for the bottom line, the figure stays true and the story stays false.
