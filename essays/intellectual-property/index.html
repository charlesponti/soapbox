The Illusion of Ownership

Intellectual property is a wall the state builds around a resource that cannot be used up — and the wall, not the idea, is the real cost

  • economics
  • intellectual-property
  • patents
  • copyright
  • innovation

Light a candle from mine and my room is no darker. The flame has not diminished because it doubled.

An idea is not bread.

Property law was built for things that run out. Two people cannot eat the same loaf, plow the same field, or drive the same tractor; consumption by one is deprivation for the other. So society drew boundaries around scarce things and called them rights. Ideas do not run out.

Use a method for planting wheat, a sequence of musical notes, a design for a bridge, and you have not taken it from anyone; the world simply has one more use of it. Thomas Jefferson said it in 1813: he who receives an idea from me receives instruction without lessening mine, as he who lights his taper at mine receives light without darkening me. The state then takes the one resource that cannot be depleted and builds a wall around it.

That is not the preservation of a natural right. It is the creation of a legal privilege that outlaws copying — the mechanism by which language, culture, and technology have always spread.

The wall does not conserve the flame; it charges admission to the light.

The case assumes invention is a discrete act of isolated genius. Innovation is incremental and collaborative; every breakthrough reorganizes existing public knowledge.

The Monopoly Masquerade

The justification is that the temporary monopoly pays for research. Without the patent, the argument runs, competitors copy the invention at once and the inventor never recoups the cost. History does not cooperate. The breakthroughs of the Industrial Revolution often happened in regions with weak or no patent law; nineteenth-century Germany had no unified patent law, and its chemical and manufacturing sectors overtook a Britain wrapped in restrictive monopolies.

The first-mover advantage is the market’s own reward. The firm that arrives first builds the brand, the distribution, the trust, and the production learning curve before any copy can catch up. In fast-moving industries, that head start matters more than any legal fence.

The first mover does not need the fence.

Software was built the same way. Linux, PostgreSQL, and Python run on no intellectual property incentive at all; they were built by open, competitive communities where value comes from implementation and service, not from hoarding code. Fashion survives design copying every season by reinventing. Recipes cannot be patented, and chefs keep innovating to keep reputations. The flame passes hand to hand and does not go out for the passing.

The modern system does not even pretend to reward innovation. Mature industries assemble defensive portfolios of broad, vague patents as mutual-assured-destruction ammunition against any startup with a better product. The startup either buys a license, sells itself, or is sued to death; a parasitic class of patent trolls buys bankrupt portfolios and lives on settlements.

The state privatizes a thin slice of the top layer of human knowledge and blocks the next builder from standing on it. Progress stalls in a licensing minefield of fees and permissions. The barrier cartelizes the industry, protecting incumbents at the expense of the new. That is the opposite of a reward for invention.

The billions spent settling trolls are a tax on building things.

The Price of the Wall

The wall stops being an economic inconvenience where the thing fenced is a life. The inventors of insulin sold their patent for one dollar in 1923, believing the drug belonged to the world. The companies that followed did not return the favor; evergreening — minor, non-therapeutic changes to extend a monopoly — has driven the price so high that diabetics ration a medicine that costs pennies to manufacture.

The dollar was not enough.

The public ledger repeats the story. The foundational breakthroughs in biology and chemistry come from publicly funded universities; the firm takes the late stage, patents the result, and charges the taxpayers who funded it.

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, millions of people in Sub-Saharan Africa died of AIDS because patents blocked cheap generic antiretrovirals, and Western pharmaceutical companies sued governments like South Africa for trying to save their citizens. The state spent its monopoly on violence to keep life-saving medicine priced above survival. When the state enforces scarcity on a thing that cannot be used up, the enforced scarcity is the only scarcity.

The digital age made the contradiction literal. A computer is a copying machine; loading a page, running a program, streaming a video is making a copy, so enforcing eighteenth-century scarcity on a medium built for abundance requires DRM locks, automated takedowns, and surveillance of every pipe.

The wall now has to watch everyone.

Abundance Without the Fence

What falls when the walls fall is not innovation but the toll. A world without intellectual property would be hyper-competitive: companies survive by constantly improving products and services instead of hiding behind legal barriers. Scientific findings — green energy, life-saving medicine — would be immediately available to build on and refine. The commons does not produce less; it produces more, faster.

This is not a utopian claim. The parts of the world that already run on sharing — language, science, the open internet — are the parts that move fastest.

None of this requires trusting the generosity of strangers. It requires trusting the arithmetic of non-rivalry. A resource that everyone can use without diminishing is a resource best used by everyone.

The patent was never the engine of progress. The passing of the flame was.
