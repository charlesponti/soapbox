Acceptance as a Status Boundary

Progressive acceptance did not end exclusion — it moved the fence from ancestry to cultural performance

  • politics
  • status
  • identity
  • tolerance
  • belonging

The fence was never removed. It was restaked.

For most of human history, the fence stood where birth placed it. You were in or out by race, religion, family, the village you were born into. The modern left rejected that fence, often for good reason. The rejection worked, and the fence did not disappear.

It just got staked somewhere new.

The New Line

A social world that forbids exclusion by inherited identity does not stop excluding. It grows extraordinarily sensitive to the symbolic: what you believe, how you speak, which words you use, where you went to school, what you watch, what you eat, which institutions you trust, which news you follow. Everyone may theoretically be welcome. Only certain kinds of difference are welcome in practice. The boundary moved from ancestry to cultural performance.

A person of any ethnicity or faith can be legible as an insider the moment they produce the right vocabulary and the right instincts. Someone demographically identical to the group can be treated as alien for the wrong attitude about immigration, gender, or policing.

The result is a strange tolerance: maximal acceptance across the approved categories of difference, intense intolerance across the ones that communicate the wrong social identity. The total amount of judgment has not fallen. Its coordinates have moved. Asking whether the left is tolerant is the wrong question; the question is tolerant of what.

The meadow in the middle has not grown; only the posts have moved.

The Evidence of Contradiction

This is where political contradiction stops being an embarrassment and becomes evidence. Beliefs that were mainstream among progressives twenty years ago can become embarrassing to voice, while positions that seemed eccentric become obligatory. The people making the change experience it as intellectual growth. The synchronization across a whole class suggests something else is operating: the social meaning of the belief changed, not the logic.

Take nations and borders: the claim that they are social constructions should, taken literally, dissolve territorial loyalty generally. The same culture that makes that claim defends Palestinian identity, indigenous sovereignty, and self-determination for subordinated peoples with real heat. The abstract rule was never the operative principle; the operative principle is power — who holds the state and who is defending themselves against it.

The same pattern repeats everywhere. Universalism appears in one debate, group particularism in another; individual autonomy is paramount in one, collective responsibility in the next. None of this requires deception. People can sincerely believe each claim and never assemble them into a system — they reason locally, and the explanation arrives after the decision.

Ideology is a stack of explanations, not a generating theory.

The Moralized Fence

The excluded are not told they are excluded. A movement that defines itself against exclusion cannot say the word, not to others and not to itself. So the rejection is moralized: the person is not rejected because the group is intolerant but because the views are harmful, ignorant, dangerous, backward. Sometimes that description is accurate, and no group is obliged to accept every proposition. A judgment can be morally justified and still be a fence.

That moralization is what hides the fence. The person enforcing the boundary experiences it not as exclusion but as ethical responsibility. The phrase we do not dislike people like you becomes we cannot tolerate ideas like yours, and from inside that sentence the distinction feels profound.

From outside it, the social effects look familiar. Certain occupations, neighborhoods, vocabularies, and tastes accumulate prestige; others lose it. The elite cultural environment develops manners: knowing the correct terminology matters, demonstrating awareness matters, identifying subtle violations matters. What emerges is not the absence of hierarchy but a new hierarchy organized around cultural fluency.

The social influence disappears into the belief itself.

No one experiences this as conformity. A person does not think, I believe this because my class believes it; they think, I believe this because I became more informed. Sometimes that is genuinely true — social transmission carries better ideas as easily as worse ones. The mechanism is just not purely philosophical.

The Reversal

Now the thought experiment. Suppose the cultural coding of several beliefs reversed overnight — universities, newspapers, artists, philanthropies all began attaching cosmopolitanism and intelligence to a different cluster of positions. How much belief would survive the switch? If beliefs rested on principles, almost all of it. If they rest on identity, far less than anyone wants to admit.

The specific beliefs would change while the identity stayed fixed. The person would still be doing the same work: occupying the position of the enlightened cosmopolitan, with the coordinates of enlightenment moved.

Which means the deepest irony is that a culture devoted to acceptance cannot perceive its own exclusions because the exclusions are encoded as acceptance. It accepts difference across the categories it decided should not matter and polices the categories that determine membership. The expansion of legal acceptance was real, and denying it would be foolish — but the story that judgment was replaced by acceptance is false, and the boundary work carries on underneath it.

Humans rarely stop judging. People change what they judge, which differences are sacred and which are shameful, and the vocabulary through which exclusion is justified. Then, because the new fence feels morally superior to the old one, people convince themselves the fence is gone. It is not gone; it is staked along the border of speech, schooling, and taste, and the meadow has never been anyone’s to claim.
