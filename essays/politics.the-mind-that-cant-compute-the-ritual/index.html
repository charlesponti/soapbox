The Mind That Can't Compute the Ritual

A mind that runs on utility treats cultural capital and monogamy as the same category — arbitrary intersubjective scripts — and cannot grasp either

  • politics
  • psychology
  • utility
  • cultural-capital
  • relationships

Set a calculator loose in a masquerade ball and it will not be confused. It will log every costume as a data error, every mask as corrupted input, and keep running, ticking the arithmetic of the evening while the masks swirl past it, subtracting the price of the wine from the value of the conversation. That is not a failure of the machine; it is the machine’s entire design, the way a toaster does not mourn the bread. The calculator was never invited to the dance, and it files that as confirmation.

This is the portrait of a particular mind — not a biography, but the architecture a certain kind of brain runs on. It processes the world as utility: durable shirts, good food, reliable partners, and every social performance that cannot be priced in that currency reads as noise, the way the masquerade reads to the machine as a sequence of corrupted inputs.

The Same Script, Twice

Cultural capital is the first thing the calculator chokes on. A restaurant or a label has no intrinsic worth; it is worth only what a specific group agrees it is worth today, and might agree is tacky tomorrow. To a processor that demands consistency, a value that shifts with the crowd is not a value — it is unstable data, and unstable data is discarded. The owner does not experience this as a failure; the owner experiences it as a refusal to be fooled.

The same filter flattens language. To this mind a word is a tool with a job; to the crowd it is a uniform that must be worn correctly, and the calculator can only score one of the two activities. It runs the same subroutine on both, and the display comes back wrong every time.

The second choke point looks unrelated and is the same error. Monogamy — the exclusivity contract — feels to the utility mind less like a real bond and more like another arbitrary script, the way others play-act the right clothes and the right political words. Strip away the romance and the contract reduces to its functional parts: the companionship that keeps the house warm, and the passion that is only available new. The processor cannot see why accessing one bucket requires destroying the other, so it runs the obvious optimization: keep the stability, acquire the passion, maximize total utility. Bentham once tried to price the whole of human feeling this way, and his felicific calculus is remembered as a machine that ran beautifully on paper and never once on people. The ledger keeps the two buckets strictly separate, and no entry ever crosses between them. It is not a rebellion against the rule; it is a failure to recognize the rule as a category worth computing.

Cultural capital and monogamy are the same species of illusion; the calculator rejects both for the same reason.

Both live in intersubjective reality, a matrix of shared agreements with no intrinsic weight. Both demand that you value something that cannot be put on a spreadsheet. Both are enforced by the tribe’s quiet expectation that you perform them sincerely, and the script has no mechanism the processor can open and inspect — no audit trail, nothing verifiable. The tribe can change its mind overnight, and the ledger has no line item for yesterday’s devotion. The utility mind sees the performance, sees the arbitrariness, and reaches the only logical conclusion: the rules shift with the needs of whoever wields them, and a mind that sees through the shift cannot respect the script.

The Contract as Risk

The same architecture re-rates the personal contract as risk management. The bond is real, but only insofar as it is useful — the couch, the Sunday market, the person you can count on. Fidelity, in this ledger, is not the promise of exclusivity; it is the question of whether you can be trusted when things get heavy, and plenty of people honor the exclusive script and cannot be counted on, which the utility mind reads as the script’s hypocrisy. Risk, not romance, is the metric, applied to the partner exactly as it is applied to the self, and the promise that cannot be verified is treated as a risk, priced into the whole arrangement.

Empathy, too, is parsed in two registers.

There is cognitive empathy — an accurate read of another’s pain, of the social cost of looking like the villain. There is low affective empathy — the pain does not reverberate in the observer’s chest, so it creates no friction against the utility of an action. The hurt is logged as an unfortunate byproduct of a broken script rather than a catastrophe. The observer is not heartless; the observer is deaf to the resonance, and the two are not the same thing.

The fortress is self-validating. When the tribe reacts to its behavior with chaos and rage, it reads the reaction as proof that the rules were arbitrary and fragile, and every broken script thickens the walls, so the machine grows safer and the ballroom colder at exactly the same rate.

The Fortress’s Tax

The person who lives in this mind calls it a logic fortress, and the name is accurate. Every wall is an argument, every gate a computation, and nothing enters that cannot be priced. The fortress is unassailable, which is exactly the problem. A bond that survives the truth is built not from consistent accounting but from accepting the inconsistent, unpriceable expectations of another person. The calculator can neither produce nor receive that, so it mistakes the ritual for the error. The tax is paid in the one currency the fortress cannot price: the permanent inability to be surprised by love, or by loss.

You cannot compute the ritual, and the ritual is the whole point. The machine keeps running, and the ball turns without it.
