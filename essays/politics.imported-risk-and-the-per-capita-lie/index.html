Imported Risk and the Per-Capita Lie

Why per-capita crime rates mean nothing to the citizen standing at the front door

  • politics
  • immigration
  • crime
  • risk
  • statistics

The wallet sits on the kitchen counter, and the money in it is gone twice. The daughter took some of it and said nothing, and the loss folded into the week, into an argument at dinner that everyone forgot by Saturday. The intruder took the same money, and the door where he came in is still the whole subject of the household — the lock, the landlord, the call to the police that went nowhere. One loss is a family matter to be settled inside the house; the other is a violation of the threshold itself, a crossing of the line the daughter was never on the wrong side of.

Per-capita crime statistics are built to erase that difference. “Immigrants commit less crime per capita than natives” is the sentence that ends every argument about imported risk, and it is statistically true and politically worthless. It treats the daughter and the intruder as two drawdowns on the same wallet, two numbers in the same ledger — the answer a bureaucrat gives because it is the only answer his spreadsheet can produce. The citizen watching the door does not see a rate. She sees a door.

Rates are for portfolios; doors are for houses.

The Wallet

The distinction is jurisdiction and obligation. A government’s first duty runs to the people already inside its house, and a society accepts a certain amount of internal crime as the cost of living among fellow nationals it cannot fully vet — a civic bond that makes the risk bearable in a way no imported risk ever is. Imported risk is different in kind, not degree: the state chose the admission, so a crime committed by someone granted entry under voluntary policy reads not as crime but as failure. No statistic launders that, because the citizen is not asking for a better number; she is asking whether the person at the door belongs there and whether the state that let him through kept its side of the contract.

The insult is that the statistic is usually even true. First-generation immigrants consistently commit less crime than native-born citizens — the immigrant paradox is a real finding, replicated across Europe and North America — and the second generation’s drift into trouble tracks poverty, school exclusion, and deprivation more than origin. Grant all of it, and the citizen is no closer to an answer: a rate that says the intruder was statistically likely to behave is a rate that has missed the question.

The deeper rule is that risk must not be imported at all — domestic crime is managed because it is the unavoidable cost of living among fellow nationals, while imported crime is different because it was preventable at the door, and the state that chose the door chose the risk. The citizen’s fury is not about the rate; it is about the choice, and a choice that was made for her.

The Absolute Math

The absolute numbers make the per-capita framing worse. Run it with friendly numbers: a thousand arrivals at one serious offense per thousand is a single victim — noise in any aggregate, a footnote in any table. Scale the same rate to a city of five hundred thousand new faces and the rate still reads as one in a thousand while five hundred real people are victimized who would not have been had those people not been present. Rate identical, victims real.

A rate reduction does not erase absolute volume, and the psychological impact of crime is cumulative, not statistical — a neighborhood does not average its fear. The victim does not file her experience under a rate; she files it under an event, and the event is what she will remember. Two crimes per ten thousand people feel different when they land on one street than when they scatter across ten, and the rate never knows the difference.

Black-swan harm does not care about your rate.

Nineteen hijackers once changed the course of the world. As a rate they were noise; as an event they rewired geopolitics, security architecture, and civil liberties for two decades. Societal trust is fragile in exactly this way — a tiny fraction of bad actors can redraw a nation’s sense of safety, and no denominator discounts the damage. Low-probability events with catastrophic impact are precisely the ones a per-capita model cannot see, which is why the model keeps promising a safety that the citizen never feels.

The Frame

The per-capita frame is not an accident; it is the only frame in which the policy looks like success. The bureaucrat evaluates migration as a utility calculation — inputs, outputs, macro-GDP, national averages — while the citizen evaluates it as a question of order and control: was this preventable, was the door enforced, does the state care more about admitting people than protecting the ones it already swore to. When a rate is offered in answer to a question about the door, it lands as a refusal, not a fact. Telling a victim her experience is statistically below average is the same insult as telling a homeowner the intruder lowered the neighborhood’s crime profile, the same refusal to meet the person at the level of the thing that happened. It is not an answer; it is an evasion wearing a decimal point.

The frame survives because the people who operate it are paid to keep it. A bureaucrat whose migration policy keeps the rates flat is a bureaucrat who looks like a success; an academic whose aggregate washes the risk into a denominator is an academic whose paper clears peer review. The citizen pays for that comfort with the only thing the frame cannot average, which is the feeling of the door.

No rate ever changed what it feels like to find the door open. Per-capita is a device for measuring crowds; the citizen lives at the threshold, and the threshold has no denominator, only the question of who is supposed to be there.
