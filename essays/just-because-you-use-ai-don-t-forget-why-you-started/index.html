Just Because You Use AI, Don't Forget Why You Started

AI removes friction. The friction was doing something important — it was telling you when the work was worth doing, when you were learning, and when you should stop.

There is a specific feeling that comes from working through something hard — not the satisfaction of finishing but the sensation of the work itself when it requires something from you. The blank page that doesn’t fill immediately. The paragraph that comes out wrong three times before it comes out right. The function that doesn’t compile and you don’t yet know why. The problem that you have to sit with long enough that your thinking about it changes. This feeling is not pleasant. It is also not optional, if the point is to get better at the thing.

Friction in creative and intellectual work is not a design flaw. It is information. It is the work telling you: this part is hard, pay attention here, this is where you are at the edge of your competence. The difficulty is the signal. The difficulty is often the lesson.

AI, used without care, removes the difficulty. It removes the blank page. It removes the wrong draft. It removes the function that doesn’t compile. It gives you, very quickly, a version of the thing you were trying to make. And if what you wanted was the thing, you have it. But if what you wanted was to become the kind of person who can make the thing, something has gone missing.

People start doing creative or technical work for different reasons, and the reasons matter more than the output. The person who starts writing because they want to think more clearly is doing something different from the person who starts writing because they want to publish. The programmer who starts coding because they want to understand how systems work is doing something different from the programmer who wants to ship features. The designer who starts drawing because they are compelled to make things is doing something different from the designer who is building a portfolio.

In the first version of each of these, the difficulty is a feature. The struggle is where the actual purpose — clearer thinking, deeper understanding, the compulsion to make — is being served. The output is a byproduct. The process is the point.

In the second version, the difficulty is an obstacle between you and the deliverable. Here, AI makes sense as a tool. Anything that reduces the distance between the intention and the output is improving the process.

The problem is that people frequently enter creative and technical work with the first motivation and gradually, through the mechanisms of professional life and platform incentives and the social rewards of output, arrive at the second. They forget what they were originally after. The process that was the point becomes the obstacle that is in the way of the product. And when AI appears and offers to remove that obstacle, it is genuinely hard to see why you shouldn’t take it.

The friction that AI removes is not random. It removes the friction that tells you things.

When writing is hard, it is often hard because the thinking behind it is unfinished. The sentence that won’t form correctly is often a sentence about a thing you don’t yet understand well enough to say simply. Forcing yourself through that sentence — writing and deleting, writing and deleting, until something true comes out — is a way of finishing the thinking. The act of writing is the act of discovering what you actually believe.

When AI writes the sentence, the thinking does not happen. The sentence appears, correct in form, plausible in content, probably adequate for most purposes. And the undone thinking remains undone, now concealed behind a sentence that reads as if it were done.

The same dynamic appears in code. When a function is difficult to write, it is often difficult because you don’t fully understand the problem you’re solving. Working through the difficulty — the wrong implementations, the debugging, the gradual building of a mental model of what the system is actually doing — produces understanding that the function alone does not. When AI produces the function on the first prompt, you have the function and you don’t have the understanding. You can ship it. You cannot, with confidence, extend it, debug it at 2am when it fails in production, or apply the lesson to the next similar problem.

There is a category of friction that is genuinely pointless and that AI should remove. The boilerplate, the mechanical transformation, the lookup that takes twenty minutes and produces nothing that wasn’t already true before the lookup. Not all difficulty is developmental. Some of it is just waste.

The task is distinguishing the two. Not all friction is feedback. But some of it is, and the friction that is feedback is disproportionately concentrated in exactly the places where AI is most capable of stepping in: the first draft, the initial solution, the shape of the thing before you know what it should be. AI is most useful at the beginning, which is exactly where the most important learning happens.

This is the specific danger. Not that AI will make you lazy in general, but that it will make you skip the part of the process that was developing you, efficiently and invisibly, while you are focusing on the output that you can measure.

The question worth sitting with is simpler than it might seem: why did you start doing this?

If the answer is “to produce the output as efficiently as possible,” then AI is an almost unambiguous gain. Use it everywhere. Reduce the time between intention and deliverable. Optimize for throughput.

If the answer is something more like “to think more clearly” or “to understand how things work” or “because the act of making something is what I am here to do” — then AI is a tool that requires much more careful calibration. It should help you where the difficulty is waste and stay out of the way where the difficulty is the point.

The difficulty is doing something. Before you ask the model to do it for you, it’s worth knowing what.

The tool accelerates your existing direction. The question is whether you still know what the direction was.
