Build, Don't Regulate

The real axis is pre-approval versus post-hoc correction, not left against right

  • politics
  • regulation
  • housing
  • nimby
  • velocity

Saturday morning on a bedroom floor, and a castle of plastic bricks has just fallen for the third time. The child rebuilds it before the argument about why it fell is finished.

Across town, a foundation pit sits behind a fence, and a committee has met for three years about the color of the rebar. Pour concrete and a bad design costs a generation, so every pour becomes a war, and the neighbors form a committee that never disbands. The material decides the politics, which is why the material is worth fighting over.

The political axis was never left and right. It is pre-approval versus post-hoc correction — whether people get to build and then fix what breaks, or must prove themselves worthy first, before a shovel touches dirt, before a drug reaches a patient, before a line of code ships to the world.

Left and right cannot see this axis; both parties campaign in the concrete world and argue about whose monument is taller.

The safety mindset treats the world as static. It wants impact studies, design reviews, public hearings, historic character reports, and a five-hundred-page zoning code, all on the theory that a mistake prevented is a mistake never made. The velocity mindset treats the world as a workshop: low barriers, fast permission, flexible reuse, and the quiet confidence that whatever fails can be torn down and rebuilt before the neighbors notice it was ever wrong.

Velocity Is Freedom

When getting something built takes ten years, fifty million dollars, and forty public hearings, a failed project is not an experiment but a monument to regret, set in stone for the grandchildren. That is why the most regulated places turn reactionary. Everyone knows whatever finally wins approval will outlast every person in the room. The rational play is to fight everything, and the irrational play is to hope.

A city that can build in six months can afford to be wrong. A bad building becomes a draft you revise, an underused space becomes an experiment that simply did not work, and a wrong call costs a season instead of a lifetime. When the cost of iteration approaches zero, the cost of a mistake approaches zero, and the panic that drives NIMBYism dissolves into ordinary housekeeping.

The same arithmetic runs through every corner of a high-friction state. A drug that takes ten years to approve kills the people who could have been saved by it in the waiting, and that death has no signature, no hearing, no headline. A solar farm held up by five years of environmental review keeps the coal plant running the whole time, and the review itself counts as virtue. Software figured this out first: eighteen months of waterfall planning and a launch that could ruin the company, replaced by daily deploys and a five-minute rollback, and nobody had to pass a law to make it so.

Deployment got cheap, so being wrong got cheap. The builders felt the difference before the politicians did.

The Regulatory Moat

The pre-approval mindset does not merely slow building; it reorders who gets to build at all. A five-hundred-page zoning code, fifteen environmental studies, three years of discretionary hearings, and two hundred thousand dollars in legal fees before breaking ground is an obstacle course that only one kind of animal can finish. That animal is not you.

A local builder with a triplex and a six-month loan cannot afford land-use attorneys. He runs out of capital while the paperwork runs out of patience. Private equity has lawyers on retainer and a decade of runway; it does not merely tolerate the friction, it loves it, because the friction deletes its competition for free and turns the whole market into its private hunting ground. Supply dries up, rents explode, and the voters who demanded the code blame greed instead of their own signatures.

This is the old alliance of Bootleggers and Baptists. The Baptists genuinely want to stop the bad thing, and the Bootleggers quietly fund the crusade because the ban is their monopoly. In modern cities the Baptists are the activists demanding affordability reviews and historic character, the Bootleggers are the institutional landlords who know every new rule kills a small rival, and the voters who think they are fighting big business supply the moral cover that eats the small fish and raises the rents.

Who Pays the Friction

Nobody votes for this outcome. They vote for a rule that stops a bad developer and never run the second-order math: the rule raises entry cost, eliminates small builders, hands the market to giants, and doubles the rent in eight years. First-order thinking writes the law; second-order thinking is what the rent bill shows up as. By then the first-order thinker is asking for more laws.

The asymmetry of blame keeps the loop spinning. A visible mistake from building something — a badly designed tower, an ugly facade — gets someone fired or sued. The invisible tragedy of things never built, families never housed, small firms never started, carries no name and no blame, so it is never corrected and never learned from.

They build the prison, hand the keys to the giant corporations, and then wonder why the walls keep rising.

Pour concrete and you fight for decades over a single pour, and the fight becomes your identity. Build with Legos and you can afford to be wrong, which is the only freedom that has ever mattered.
